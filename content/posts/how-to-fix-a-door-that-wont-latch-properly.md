A door that won't latch is one of those problems that starts as a minor annoyance and quietly becomes a source of daily frustration. You push the door closed, it bounces back. You lift the handle a little, angle your shoulder, try again. Eventually you just leave it open because fighting with it isn't worth the energy. If that's where you are right now, you're in good company. I've seen this problem in $80,000 bungalows and $800,000 new builds. It's incredibly common, and in probably 80% of cases, you can fix it yourself in under an hour.

Let's talk about what's actually happening.

## Why Doors Stop Latching (It's Almost Never the Latch Itself)

Here's what I tell people when they call me about this: the latch bolt is fine. The hardware is almost always fine. The problem is alignment. Specifically, the latch bolt and the strike plate hole on the door frame have drifted out of alignment, usually by less than half an inch. That small gap is all it takes.

The question is why they drifted. And there are really three culprits.

**Seasonal movement.** Wood swells in humid summers and contracts in dry winters. A door that latches perfectly in February can fight you every August. If your problem is seasonal, you might not need a permanent fix -- you need to understand the pattern and decide how much it bothers you.

**Settling.** Houses move. Foundations shift slightly over years and decades. The framing around a door opening can rack, even a little, and that changes everything about how the door sits in its frame. This is especially common in houses built before 1980, and in any house that's had foundation work done.

**Hinge issues.** This is the most underdiagnosed cause. Loose hinge screws let the door sag at the hinge side, which drops the latch side. The latch bolt ends up hitting below the strike plate hole. I've fixed dozens of doors just by tightening three screws and calling it a day.

So before you do anything else, close the door slowly and watch where the latch bolt meets the strike plate. Is it hitting too low? Too high? Missing to one side? That observation tells you exactly what fix you need.

## Start With the Hinges

Seriously. Before you touch the strike plate or break out the chisel, check every hinge screw on the door. Open the door fully and try to wiggle it. Any movement at the hinges at all? Tighten every screw you can.

Here's the catch: on older doors, the screw holes are often stripped. The screw goes in, spins, and doesn't actually tighten. This is so common it deserves its own fix. The standard move is to pull the screw out, stuff two or three toothpicks into the hole with a dab of wood glue (Elmer's or Titebond, doesn't matter), let it dry for an hour, snap off the excess, and drive the screw back in. It sounds too simple to work. It works.

If the screws are stripped beyond the toothpick trick, upgrade to 3-inch screws. Standard hinge screws are usually 3/4 inch and only grab the door casing, not the actual stud. A 3-inch screw goes all the way into the structural framing and holds like it means it. This one swap has fixed dramatic door sag on jobs where I thought I'd need to rehang the whole thing.

## The Strike Plate Fix (The One You Probably Need)

Once you've handled the hinges and the door still isn't latching, it's time to look at the strike plate. This is the metal plate mortised into the door frame with a hole (or two holes) that the latch bolt is supposed to snap into.

The diagnostic step is simple. Grab a piece of colored chalk, lipstick, or even a grease pencil. Coat the end of the latch bolt with it, then close the door and press it firmly against the frame without engaging the latch. Open it back up and look at where the chalk transferred onto the strike plate. That mark tells you exactly how far off you are and in which direction.

**If the latch is hitting low on the strike plate**, you're likely dealing with a sagging door. Fix the hinges first, then reassess.

**If the offset is small (1/8 to 3/8 inch in any direction)**, you can fix this without removing the strike plate. Use a metal file to enlarge the strike plate hole in the direction you need. A Nicholson 6-inch hand file from any hardware store runs about $12 and is the right tool for this. Take it slow, test frequently, and stop when the bolt catches.

**If the offset is larger**, you need to reposition the strike plate entirely. This means unscrewing the plate, chiseling out more of the mortise in the new direction, and patching the old mortise location with wood filler (DAP Plastic Wood works fine) before you reinstall. It takes about 45 minutes and requires a sharp 1-inch chisel. Not a big deal. I'd say this is a confident beginner job.

One thing I want to be clear about: don't skip the mortise work and just let the strike plate sit proud of the door frame surface. I see this shortcut constantly. The door will never seat fully, you'll always feel a little bump when you close it, and the fix won't hold as long. Do it right.

## When It's Actually the Door Frame (Or the Door Itself)

Sometimes the gap between the door and the frame is visibly uneven. You'll see a wide gap at the top latch corner and almost none at the bottom, or vice versa. This usually means something structural has shifted. Could be the framing, could be the foundation. If you're in a newer house and this happened quickly, pay attention.

For moderate racking where the door still fits in the frame without binding, repositioning the hinges can help. You can add a thin cardboard shim (cut from a cereal box, genuinely) behind one side of a hinge leaf to adjust the door's angle slightly. It's painstaking work that requires trial and error, but it's free and doesn't require major surgery.

If the door is binding and sticking badly before it even reaches the latch, the door itself may need to be planed. You can do this yourself with a hand plane or a belt sander, but I'll be honest: if you've never planed a door before, the risk of taking off too much is real. On a nice solid wood door, I'd bring in a carpenter. On a hollow-core interior door from Home Depot that cost $60, go for it.

One scenario where I always tell people to call a pro: if you're seeing the same door-and-latch problem showing up in multiple rooms, or if doors and windows throughout the house are sticking or not closing right, that's a conversation to have with a structural engineer, not a contractor. It might be nothing, but it might be foundation movement that needs to be caught early. I've been on jobs where a homeowner had been fighting multiple sticky doors for two years and had no idea there was a foundation issue underneath it all.

## What This Should Cost You

If you're DIYing, you're looking at basically nothing. A metal file, some toothpicks, maybe a new strike plate if yours is damaged. Budget $20 and an hour.

If you hire a handyman for a simple strike plate adjustment or hinge tightening, you're looking at $75 to $150 depending on your market. That's one to two hours of labor.

Rehinging a door that's truly sagging or warped, done by a carpenter, typically runs $150 to $300. If the door frame itself needs repair or shimming due to settling, that can push to $400 to $600, especially if drywall finishing is involved afterward.

Replacing the door and frame entirely -- which is almost never necessary for a latching problem -- starts around $500 installed for a basic interior door and goes up fast.

The honest version: if you've tried the hinge fix and the strike plate adjustment and the door still won't latch, spend the $150 on a handyman before you start throwing money at a new door. Nine times out of ten they'll find something simple that was just slightly outside what you could see.

## FAQ

### Why does my door latch in winter but not in summer?

Wood expands when it absorbs moisture in humid summer months, which shifts the door enough that the latch bolt no longer lines up with the strike plate hole. This is extremely common in older homes with solid wood doors and frames. Either widen the strike plate hole slightly with a file to accommodate the swelled position, or plane a small amount off the door edge.

### Can I fix a door that won't latch without removing the strike plate?

Yes, in many cases. If the misalignment is minor (under 3/8 inch), you can file the strike plate opening larger in the direction you need without removing it from the frame. Mark where the latch bolt is hitting first so you know which direction to file.

### My door latch clicks but the door still swings open. What's wrong?

The latch bolt is probably engaging but not deeply enough to hold. This usually means the strike plate hole is too shallow or partially obstructed, or the door has shifted so the bolt only catches the edge of the opening. Try the chalk test to see exactly where the bolt is landing, then file or reposition the strike plate accordingly.

### How do I know if my door problem is a foundation issue and not just a normal fix?

Look for patterns. A single sticky door in summer is normal wood movement. Multiple doors and windows sticking or not closing throughout the house, especially if it came on relatively quickly or got noticeably worse, is worth a professional eye. Cracks in drywall near door corners are another sign to take seriously.

### What's the easiest way to fix stripped hinge screw holes?

Fill the hole with two or three toothpicks and a drop of wood glue, let it cure for an hour, snap off the toothpicks flush, and re-drive the screw. It's low-tech and it genuinely works. If the door is very heavy or the sagging is significant, switch to 3-inch screws that reach the wall stud instead.
