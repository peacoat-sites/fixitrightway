Your shower won’t stop dripping even after you turn it all the way off. That slow, steady drip isn’t just annoying,  it can waste hundreds of gallons of water every month and quietly drive up your water bill. If your **shower drips when off**, the good news is that this is one of the most common plumbing problems homeowners face, and it’s almost always fixable without calling a plumber. In this guide, we’ll walk you through exactly what causes the drip and how to stop it for good.

## Why Does Your Shower Keep Dripping After You Turn It Off?

Before you grab your tools, it helps to understand what’s actually causing the problem. A dripping shower almost always comes down to worn or damaged parts inside the faucet handle or valve assembly.

Here are the most common culprits:

  * **Worn washers or O-rings**,  These rubber seals compress every time you use the shower. Over time, they degrade and stop creating a watertight seal.
  * **Damaged cartridge**,  Many modern showers use a cartridge-style valve. When the cartridge wears out, water seeps past it even when the handle is off.
  * **Faulty ceramic disc**,  Some faucets use ceramic discs instead of washers. Cracks or mineral buildup can cause them to fail.
  * **High water pressure**,  Excessively high pressure can force water past even a healthy valve seal.
  * **Corroded valve seat**,  The valve seat connects the faucet and spout. If it corrodes, leaks develop around the washer area.

Knowing which type of faucet you have,  single-handle, double-handle, or cartridge-based,  will determine which repair steps apply to you.

## Tools and Materials You Will Need

Gathering everything before you start saves time and frustration. Most of these items are inexpensive and available at any hardware store.

  * Flathead and Phillips screwdrivers
  * Adjustable wrench or basin wrench
  * Needle-nose pliers
  * Allen wrench set (hex keys)
  * Replacement cartridge, washers, or O-rings (match your faucet brand)
  * Plumber’s grease (silicone-based)
  * White vinegar (for mineral buildup)
  * Towels or rags
  * Bucket or shallow pan
  * Flashlight or headlamp

If you’re not sure which replacement parts to buy, take a photo of your faucet brand and model number at the hardware store. Staff there can point you to the exact cartridge or washer kit you need.

**Pro Tip:** Before purchasing replacement parts, remove your existing cartridge or washer first and bring it to the hardware store. Matching the part in person is far more reliable than going by model numbers alone, especially with older fixtures.

## Step 1: Shut Off the Water Supply

This is the most important step,  never skip it. Working on a shower valve without shutting off the water first will result in a very wet bathroom.

**⚠️ Safety:** Always turn off the water supply before opening any faucet valve or removing any plumbing component. If your shower does not have a dedicated shut-off valve, turn off the main water supply to your home. Skipping this step can cause flooding and serious water damage.

Most showers have an access panel behind the wall, or individual shut-off valves located in a nearby closet or utility space. Turn the valve clockwise until it stops. Then turn on the shower handle to release any pressure and confirm the water is fully off before proceeding.

Once the water is off, place a towel on the floor of the shower and put your bucket or pan under the work area to catch any residual drips.

## Step 2: Remove the Handle and Access the Valve

With the water safely off, it’s time to open up the faucet. The exact process varies slightly by faucet type, but the general steps are similar.

Start by locating the decorative cap on your handle,  it’s usually a small plastic or metal disc in the center. Pop it off gently with a flathead screwdriver. Underneath, you’ll find a screw holding the handle in place.

Use the appropriate screwdriver or Allen wrench to remove that screw, then pull the handle straight off. If it feels stuck, wiggle it gently,  do not force it. Some handles need a handle puller tool if they’ve been on for many years.

Once the handle is off, you may see a decorative trim plate or escutcheon. Remove any screws holding it in place and set it aside. You should now be looking at the valve cartridge or stem assembly inside the wall.

## Step 3: Inspect and Replace the Cartridge or Washer

This is where the actual repair happens. What you find inside will guide the rest of your fix.

**For cartridge-style faucets:** You’ll see a cylindrical cartridge held in place by a retaining clip or nut. Use needle-nose pliers to remove the retaining clip, then pull the cartridge straight out. Inspect it for cracks, warping, or visible wear. In most cases, replacing it entirely is the best approach,  cartridges are inexpensive and a fresh one will give you years of drip-free use.

**For compression-style faucets:** Unscrew the packing nut using your wrench, then remove the stem. At the bottom of the stem, you’ll find a rubber washer held by a brass screw. If it’s flattened, cracked, or hardened, that’s your problem. Replace it with an identical washer, apply a thin coat of plumber’s grease, and reassemble.

**For ceramic disc faucets:** Remove the disc cartridge and inspect the ceramic discs. Clean them thoroughly with white vinegar to remove mineral deposits. If they’re cracked, replace the entire disc cartridge.

Always apply a thin layer of silicone-based plumber’s grease to any new rubber parts before reassembling. This extends the life of the washer or O-ring and helps everything seat properly.

## Step 4: Reassemble the Faucet

Once your new cartridge or washer is in place, reassemble everything in reverse order. Start with the cartridge or stem, then attach the retaining clip or packing nut, then the escutcheon plate, and finally the handle.

Make sure the cartridge is oriented correctly,  most cartridges have a tab or notch that aligns with a slot in the valve body. Installing it backward is a common mistake that will cause the hot and cold to be reversed or result in continued leaking.

Tighten screws snugly but do not overtighten. Over-tightening can crack plastic components or strip screw holes, making your next repair much harder.

## Step 5: Turn the Water Back On and Test

With everything reassembled, it’s time to see if your fix worked. Slowly turn the water supply back on,  don’t open it all at once. A sudden surge of pressure can loosen connections or damage new parts.

Once the supply is fully open, turn the shower handle on and off several times. Watch the showerhead closely for drips after you turn it off. Let it sit for five minutes to be sure.

If the dripping has stopped, congratulations,  you just fixed your shower! If it’s still dripping, double-check that the cartridge is properly seated and that all connections are tight. In some cases, the valve seat itself may need resurfacing or replacement, which may require a valve seat wrench or professional help.

## When to Call a Plumber

Most dripping showers can be fixed with the steps above, but there are situations where calling a licensed plumber is the right call. Don’t hesitate to get professional help if you encounter any of the following:

  * The valve body itself is cracked or corroded
  * You can’t identify the faucet brand or find replacement parts
  * Water is leaking behind the wall, not just from the showerhead
  * The shut-off valve is seized and won’t turn
  * You’re not comfortable working with plumbing at all

A plumber can typically fix a dripping shower valve in under an hour. The peace of mind is worth it if you’re feeling unsure about any step in this process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much water does a dripping shower waste?

A shower that drips just 10 times per minute wastes more than 500 gallons of water per year. A faster drip,  which is more common with a failing cartridge,  can waste well over 3,000 gallons annually. Fixing the leak promptly saves both water and money on your utility bills.

### Can I fix a dripping shower without turning off the main water supply?

If your shower has a dedicated shut-off valve,  usually found in an access panel behind the shower wall,  you can use that instead of shutting off the whole house. However, if no dedicated valve exists, you must turn off the main supply. Never attempt to open a valve body with water pressure active.

### How long do shower cartridges typically last?

Most shower cartridges last between 10 and 20 years with normal use. Water quality plays a big role,  hard water with high mineral content tends to wear cartridges out faster. If your shower is more than 15 years old and starts dripping, replacing the cartridge proactively is a smart and affordable maintenance move.
