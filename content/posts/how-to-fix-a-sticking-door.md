A door that sticks in summer and swings free in winter isn't broken. It's telling you something. Most homeowners sand it down, paint it, and then spend the next rainy season fighting it again because they fixed the symptom without understanding the cause. That cycle ends here.

## Why Your Door Is Actually Sticking

Sticking doors have three root causes, and the fix depends entirely on which one you're dealing with.

**Wood movement.** This is the most common culprit, and it's seasonal. Wood expands when it absorbs moisture and shrinks when it dries out. A door that sticks only in humid months and loosens up by October is a wood movement problem. If you let a contractor talk you into planing the door in July, you'll have a gap in January.

**Foundation or framing shift.** If the door sticks year-round, rubs in a diagonal pattern, or the gap around it is visibly uneven, your house has moved. This happens in older homes, homes with clay-heavy soil, or anywhere that had a wet winter followed by a dry summer. The door isn't the problem. The frame is.

**Loose or failing hardware.** Sagging hinges cause more sticking doors than most people realize. A 3-pound door on worn hinges pulls the latch side up and the hinge side down. The door binds at the top corner opposite the hinges. Before you touch sandpaper, check whether the hinge screws are snug.

Diagnosing correctly before you pick up a tool saves you hours. I've seen homeowners plane a perfectly good door down to firewood because they never stopped to figure out why it was sticking.

## How to Diagnose the Problem in 10 Minutes

You need a dollar bill, a flashlight, and your eyes. That's it.

Close the door fully and look at the gap around the perimeter. It should be roughly consistent, about 1/8 inch on the sides and top, slightly more at the bottom. Uneven gaps tell you the story immediately.

**If the gap is wide at the top latch side and tight at the bottom latch side:** the hinge side has dropped, almost always from loose hinges or a settling frame.

**If it's tight along the entire top edge:** the door has swollen from moisture, or the top of the frame has dropped.

**If it rubs diagonally:** foundation movement. The frame is racking.

Now use the dollar bill. Slide it around the door edge while the door is closed. Where it drags or can't move at all is exactly where the door is binding. Mark those spots with a pencil on the door edge. This takes two minutes and gives you a precise map.

Finally, grab the door handle and physically lift up on it. If the door stops binding when you lift, your hinges are loose or worn. Check the hinge screws. If any of them spin without tightening, the hole has stripped out.

## The Fixes, Ranked from Easiest to Most Involved

Work through these in order. Most sticking doors are solved at step one or two.

**1. Tighten and reinforce the hinges.** Remove one screw at a time from each hinge. If a screw spins, the wood has stripped. The fix: remove the screw, stuff the hole with two or three wooden toothpicks coated in wood glue, let it dry for an hour, then reinstall the screw. The toothpicks give the threads something to bite. This costs under a dollar and solves the problem permanently about 40% of the time.

If the hinge itself is loose or the mortise has widened, consider replacing standard 1.5-inch screws with 3-inch screws that reach into the stud behind the door frame. Those are the real workhorses.

**2. Adjust the strike plate.** Sometimes the door doesn't stick so much as it doesn't latch without force. The strike plate may need to move up, down, or out by a few millimeters. Hold a piece of chalk or lipstick on the latch bolt, close the door, and check where it marks the strike plate. If the mark is off-center, you can enlarge the strike plate mortise slightly with a chisel or a rotary tool. A $3 tube of lipstick has diagnosed more door problems than most tools I own.

**3. Lubricate the hinge pins.** Dry, corroded hinge pins create friction that feels like the door itself is binding. Pull the pins, wipe them down, coat them with petroleum jelly or a dry PTFE lubricant, and reinstall. Takes five minutes.

**4. Plane or sand the binding edge.** If wood movement is confirmed and the door swells consistently in humid months, you can remove material, but do it in the humid season when the door is fully swollen. Mark your bind points, remove the door, and take off 1/16 inch at a time with a hand plane, belt sander, or circular saw with a guide. Always seal the planed edge with paint or primer immediately. Bare wood wicks moisture and defeats the purpose.

**5. Adjust or shim the hinges.** If the door is sagging, you can sometimes bring it back into square by shimming the lower hinge out slightly. Cut a piece of cardboard to the exact size of the hinge mortise, place it behind the hinge leaf, and reinstall. This pushes the bottom of the door away from the frame and can correct a surprising amount of sag. It sounds rudimentary. It works.

## Planing a Door: Step-by-Step

When material removal is genuinely necessary, here's how to do it without ruining the door.

**What you need:** Hand plane or belt sander, sawhorses, pencil, primer or paint, 120-grit sandpaper, safety glasses.

1. Mark all binding spots with pencil while the door is hung and fully swollen. Be specific.
2. Remove the door. Take both hinge-side pins out and lift the door free. Have a helper if the door is solid core, which can weigh 80 to 100 pounds.
3. Set the door on sawhorses, binding edge facing up.
4. If using a hand plane, set the blade to a shallow cut, about 1/32 inch. Plane with the grain whenever possible. Check your pencil marks constantly.
5. If using a belt sander, use 60-grit to remove material and 120-grit to smooth. Keep the sander moving. A stationary belt sander will dig a hollow.
6. Remove less than you think you need. You can always take more off. You can't put it back.
7. Test-fit the door before it's off the sawhorses by holding it in the opening. Adjust as needed.
8. Before rehinging, prime or paint every bare edge. Every single one. This is where most people skip and pay for it in the next rainy season.
9. Rehang and check the gap uniformly.

Total time: 1 to 3 hours depending on how much material you're removing and how many times you test-fit.

## When the Problem Is Your Foundation, Not Your Door

A racking door frame means the structural opening has changed shape. The door is a rectangle. The opening is now a parallelogram. No amount of sanding fixes that.

Signs you're dealing with frame or foundation movement: sticking that developed suddenly, cracks in the drywall above the door corners, doors in multiple rooms sticking at the same time, or a gap that runs diagonally from one corner to another.

If you see one or two of these, call a structural engineer or an experienced contractor for an assessment before you touch the door. In my experience, homeowners spend $200 planing and refinishing a door and then spend $8,000 on foundation work anyway six months later. The door was a warning sign.

Small, old settlement that has stabilized is different from active movement. A house built in 1920 has usually finished settling. If the sticking is new and progressive, that's the thing to worry about.

Foundation repair costs vary wildly: $500 for minor crack injection up to $15,000 or more for pier installation. An inspection typically runs $300 to $500 and tells you whether you're dealing with cosmetic settling or a real structural issue. Worth every dollar.

## Exterior Doors vs. Interior Doors: The Differences That Matter

Everything above applies broadly to both, but exterior doors have a few extra considerations.

Exterior doors see more temperature swings, direct sun, rain, and humidity. A south-facing exterior door can expand and contract more than half an inch over the course of a year in some climates. If your exterior door sticks seasonally and you're in a humid climate, the most durable fix is often preventive: strip and repaint the door edges every few years to maintain a continuous moisture barrier.

Exterior doors also carry more hardware weight and have weather stripping that adds friction. If the door seals tightly in winter but sticks badly in summer, weather stripping may be compressing unevenly or the door has bowed slightly. A bowed door needs a different approach: adjusting the strike plate depth, replacing the weather stripping, or in severe cases, replacing the door itself.

Interior doors are usually hollow-core and lighter, which means hinges last longer and swelling is typically more modest. The same diagnosis and fix sequence applies, but you'll generally deal with smaller adjustments and faster results.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I know if my sticking door is a DIY fix or a contractor job?

If it sticks only seasonally, responds to hinge tightening, or has a clear rubbing spot on one edge, it's DIY territory. If the gap is visibly uneven, the frame looks out of square, or drywall cracks have appeared near the corners, call a contractor. The door may be symptomatic of something bigger.

### Can I fix a sticking door without removing it?

Often, yes. Hinge tightening, hinge pin lubrication, strike plate adjustment, and minor sanding with a hand plane or sanding block can all be done with the door in place. You only need to remove the door if you're planing a significant amount of material from the latch edge or the full top edge.

### My hinge screws keep stripping out. Is there a better fix than toothpicks?

Toothpicks with wood glue work for light- to medium-duty situations. For exterior doors or solid-core interior doors, use wooden golf tees instead. They're thicker and fill the hole more completely. For seriously damaged holes, a golf tee plus wood glue and then a 3-inch screw into the stud is as permanent as it gets without full frame repair.

### Will painting my door help stop it from sticking?

Painting helps by sealing the wood against moisture absorption. It won't fix an already-swollen door, but it's genuinely effective as prevention. The key is sealing all six faces of the door, including top and bottom edges that most people ignore. An unsealed bottom edge can absorb enough moisture to cause noticeable swelling on its own.

### How much does it cost to have a contractor fix a sticking door?

A simple hinge adjustment or minor planing runs $75 to $150 for a service call with an experienced handyman. Full door removal, planing, rehanging, and refinishing can run $200 to $400 depending on your area. If the frame needs adjustment or the door needs replacement, add $300 to $800. Foundation issues are a separate budget line entirely.
