Nearly 7 in 10 American homeowners are sitting on a pile of unfinished repairs [right now](/how-to-shop-for-materials-right-now-and-beat-tariff-price-hikes/). That's not a rough estimate. Hippo's 2026 Homeowner Anxiety Report, released in May, put the number at 69% ,  and what surprised me when I read through the full report was how honest people were about *why*. It's not ignorance. Most homeowners know exactly what's wrong with their house. They're just paralyzed by the list, the cost, or both. And with summer finally here, a lot of people are ready to stop avoiding it. The question isn't whether to start. It's where.

The answer is not "whatever's cheapest." I'll be honest: that instinct is how a $400 plumbing fix becomes a $12,000 subfloor replacement. Urgency and cost are different axes entirely, and treating your backlog like a to-do list you can just work through top to bottom is one of the most expensive mistakes I see homeowners make.

## The Urgency Tier You Actually Need

Not all deferred repairs are equal. Some sit quietly for years. Others are quietly destroying your house right now. Here's the framework I use when I walk a homeowner through a backlog.

Tier 1 is anything involving water or electricity. These are your active threats. A slow roof leak isn't "minor" ,  it's feeding mold, rotting sheathing, and potentially wicking down wall cavities where you'll never see it until demo. Same with a dripping supply line under a sink. Water finds the path of least resistance, and that path is always toward something expensive. Electrical issues ,  flickering circuits, breakers that trip repeatedly, outlets that feel warm ,  are fire risk, full stop. These get addressed first. No exceptions.

Tier 2 is structural or envelope issues: foundation cracks that are actively widening, exterior wood rot that's exposed to the weather, soffits or fascia that are letting animals in. These aren't emergencies today, but they will be by next spring if you ignore them through another winter.

Tier 3 is everything else: HVAC tune-ups, caulking, deck refinishing, cosmetic damage. Important, but they wait if your money and bandwidth are limited.

## Where the Deferred Backlog Is Actually Piling Up

The Hippo data tells a clear story about what homeowners are dodging. Plumbing tops the list at 25%, followed by roof or foundation work at 22%, and electrical at 21%. What's striking is that these three categories, the ones people are most likely to put off, are also the three where delay causes the most catastrophic cost escalation.

There's a psychological reason plumbing leads. A lot of plumbing problems are invisible or intermittent. The toilet runs. The [water pressure](/how-to-fix-low-water-pressure-in-your-home/)'s a little low. Nothing is flooding. So it waits. But slow leaks under slabs, inside walls, or at supply connections are doing continuous damage. I've pulled drywall on "minor plumbing issues" and found studs that looked like wet cardboard.

The cost reality is sobering. Nearly half of homeowners ,  45% ,  estimate they need $5,000 or more just to get current. Fifteen percent say $20,000+. And average DIY project spend in Q1 2026 hit $5,368, up roughly 35% from a year earlier, according to data from The Farnsworth Group. That jump tells me [material costs](/how-tariff-driven-material-costs-are-changing-what-you-should-diy/) and contractor rates are still elevated, which means every month you wait on a Tier 1 repair is a month where both the damage and the fix are getting more expensive simultaneously.

## The DIY Trap: Why 87% Are Doing the Wrong Projects

Here's something that should be uncomfortable: a July 2026 Mecklenburg Times survey found 94% of homeowners are planning DIY work this year, making 2026 one of the most active home improvement years on record. That sounds great. But 87% of them are limiting themselves to cosmetic projects: paint, fixtures, landscaping, hardware swaps.

I get it. Painting a bedroom feels productive. It looks better immediately. Nobody's going to electrocute themselves. But if your attic has active water intrusion and you spent the weekend replacing cabinet pulls, you've done nothing to stop the bleeding.

Here's a cleaner way to think about the DIY/hire split for common backlog items:

| Repair | DIY Feasibility | Typical Pro Cost | When to DIY |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Toilet flapper/fill valve | High | $150-$250 | Almost always |
| Leaking supply line under sink | High | $150-$300 | If you're comfortable with shutoffs |
| Roof shingle patch (3-5 shingles) | Moderate | $400-$800 | Single story, you're comfortable on ladders |
| Full roof replacement | Low | $8,000-$18,000 | Rarely ,  liability and warranty issues |
| GFCI outlet replacement | Moderate | $100-$200 | If circuit is dead first |
| Panel work / breaker replacement | Low | $300-$800+ | Almost never ,  permit and safety risk |
| Foundation crack injection (hairline) | Moderate | $500-$1,200 | Only for non-structural cracks |
| Subfloor repair | Low-Moderate | $1,500-$4,000 | Depends heavily on scope |

The DIY win zone is real, but it's narrower than most people think when they're staring down a legitimate deferred backlog.

## The Stress Factor Is Real, and It's Costing You

More than 1 in 3 homeowners say their home's repair state is either their biggest source of stress or a major contributor to it. That's from the Hippo report, and I believe it completely. I've seen homeowners paralyzed by a list of 15 things, doing none of them, because they can't figure out where to start. The backlog becomes a psychological object, not a repair list.

The fix for this, practically speaking, is triage over perfection. You do not need to resolve everything this summer. You need to neutralize your Tier 1 threats before fall, when weather closes the window on exterior work and heating season starts. Get one roofing contractor out for a real inspection, not a free "estimate" that's really a sales call. Get a licensed plumber to assess the issues you've been ignoring. Electrical: if you have an older panel, aluminum wiring, or any signs of heat at outlets, that's a phone call this week.

The homeowners who feel best about their houses aren't the ones who finished everything. They're the ones who stopped the active bleeding and have a realistic plan for the rest.
