Most coverage of duct sealing spends three paragraphs telling you [air leaks](/how-to-seal-air-leaks-around-electrical-outlets/) are bad, then hands you a tube of caulk and wishes you luck. That's not enough. Duct leakage is one of the most consequential hidden problems in residential buildings, and it's also one of the most frequently botched DIY repairs because people use the wrong materials, seal the wrong spots, or skip a pressure test that would tell them whether any of it worked.

Here's the uncomfortable number: the U.S. Department of Energy estimates that the average home loses 20 to 30 percent of conditioned air through duct leaks. On a $250/month cooling bill, that's $50 to $75 a month leaving through gaps in your attic or crawlspace. Every month. I've seen homes where the real figure was closer to 40 percent, usually because the original installer used cloth duct tape (which fails completely within a few years) and nobody touched it again.

The fix is straightforward. The execution is where people go wrong.

## What You're Actually Sealing (and Why Location Matters)

Duct systems leak in predictable places. The joints between duct sections, the connections at registers and grilles, the plenum connections at the air handler itself, and any point where a duct passes through a floor, ceiling, or wall framing. The air handler plenum is often the single worst offender. That's the metal box directly attached to your furnace or air handler unit, and the seams on it are almost always undersized and often never sealed at installation.

The reason location matters: ducts in conditioned space (inside your thermal envelope, basically inside the house) that leak are annoying and wasteful. Ducts in unconditioned space, like a vented attic in Phoenix or a crawlspace in Georgia, are genuinely expensive. You're dumping 75-degree air into a 130-degree attic and paying your HVAC system to pump it there. That's where the real money bleeds out.

Before you buy anything, walk your attic or crawlspace with a flashlight on a day when your HVAC is running. Hold your hand near every joint, every collar, every flex duct connection. You'll feel the leaks. Some will be dramatic. I did this in my own house after we moved in, found a 4-inch gap at the plenum where the previous owner had disconnected a duct and simply never reconnected it. That single gap was costing us roughly $80/month in wasted cooling. Found it in five minutes with my hand.

## Mastic vs. Foil Tape: Take a Position

Everyone online hedges this. I won't.

Mastic sealant is the right answer for sealing duct joints. It's a fiber-reinforced paste (water-based, usually gray or white) that you brush or trowel onto joints and seams. It stays flexible, doesn't dry out and crack, and lasts the life of the duct if applied correctly. The product I've used for years is Hardcast 321, about $18/gallon at most HVAC supply houses. You can also find RCD Corporation Mastic or Gardner Bender's equivalent at big-box stores. For larger gaps (anything over 1/4 inch), you embed fiberglass [mesh tape](/mesh-tape-vs-paper-tape-for-drywall-seams/) into the wet mastic before it dries. That mesh is the part most DIYers skip and regret.

UL-181 rated foil tape (aluminum foil tape, not cloth "duct tape") is acceptable on rigid sheet metal ducts when the surface is clean and you press it on properly. It works. But it's strictly a second choice because the adhesive eventually loosens with heat cycling, and in dirty or dusty attics the bond never fully achieves in the first place.

Standard silver cloth duct tape has no place on ductwork. It fails in a few years under heat. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory did testing on this in the late 1990s that's still cited today: cloth duct tape was the worst-performing product tested, failing faster than everything else. Don't use it.

## The Actual Repair Process

Clean surfaces are everything. Mastic won't adhere to dusty, oily, or wet metal. Wipe each joint down with a damp rag, let it dry, then brush on a coat of mastic thick enough to cover the seam with no metal showing through, roughly 1/8 inch. On big gaps, press your fiberglass mesh into wet mastic, let that dry 30-60 minutes, then add a second coat over the top. That second coat is the one most people skip because the first coat looks fine. Don't skip it.

For flex duct connections (the corrugated silver tubing most homes built since the 1980s have everywhere), the process is slightly different. The flex duct slips over a metal collar and is supposed to be secured with a clamp and mastic. What I usually find instead: a couple wraps of cloth tape, half-peeled. Strip that off, tighten a proper worm-gear clamp over the inner liner of the flex duct onto the metal collar, then mastic over the whole joint. Then tape the outer insulation jacket back down with foil tape. Two layers, not one.

The air handler plenum gets the most mastic. Every seam, every screw hole (yes, screws leave gaps), every point where a collar penetrates the plenum wall. It's tedious but this is where you get the most return.

## When to Call Someone Instead

Aeroseal is a different category entirely. It's a pressurized polymer mist that gets blown through your duct system from the inside, sealing holes and gaps from within. It reaches leaks you can't access, including ducts buried in wall cavities and joints hidden behind insulation. A reader in Sacramento, Mark, emailed me after he'd spent two weekends mastic-sealing everything he could reach and his post-work duct blaster test still showed 18% leakage. He got Aeroseal done; his final leakage dropped to 4%. The cost was $1,847 for his 1,600 sq ft house.

Aeroseal is certified by EPA Energy Star and has been validated in multiple studies. Current as of August 2026, the national average cost runs $1,500 to $2,500 depending on house size and local contractor pricing. It's not a replacement for fixing obvious large gaps first; it's best used after you've handled everything accessible, then let it chase down what's left.

Call a pro, not DIY, if:
- Your air handler or furnace is old enough that touching the plenum risks disturbing asbestos tape (common in systems installed before 1980)
- Your ducts run through finished ceilings or interior walls with no access panels
- You've already sealed everything visible and still have high energy bills

## Proving It Worked

Here's the part most DIY guides leave out entirely: verification. You can spend a weekend in a dusty attic and have no idea whether your work moved the needle. A duct blaster test (performed by an energy auditor or HVAC tech with the right equipment) measures total duct leakage as a percentage of system airflow. Expect to pay $150 to $300 for a standalone test, or it's often included in a full home energy audit.

Before-and-after testing matters. A 3,500 sq ft home in Austin, sealed by the homeowner over two weekends with mastic and mesh, went from 28% duct leakage to 11% on a duct blaster test. The owner's summer cooling bill dropped from an average of $312/month to $219/month. That's $1,116/year saved. Materials cost him $94.

If you can't afford a formal test, the poor man's version is a smoke pencil near registers while the system runs, or simply tracking your utility bills month-over-month and year-over-year. Imprecise, but better than nothing.

One small detail that'll save you frustration: mastic takes 24 to 48 hours to fully cure, longer in high humidity. Don't run the HVAC system at full blast the day you apply it. Give it overnight minimum. I learned this by not giving it overnight, and pulled a freshly mastic'd joint right apart because the pressure stressed it before it set.
