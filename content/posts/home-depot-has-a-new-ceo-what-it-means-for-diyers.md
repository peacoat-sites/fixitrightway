Home Depot just reshuffled its entire top leadership on the same day it reported Q3 earnings that beat Wall Street expectations, and the timing matters if you're a homeowner who shops there regularly. On November 17, 2026, the company announced that Richard McPhail, the former CFO, would take over as CEO effective November 23, replacing Ted Decker, who stepped down after returning from medical leave. Simultaneously, 41-year company veteran Ann-Marie Campbell was named President and COO, and Jordan Broggi moved into the CFO seat. That's three major executive changes in one announcement. For a retailer this size, that kind of leadership shift signals a real change in direction, not just a seat swap.

The backdrop makes this more interesting for DIYers specifically. Home Depot's Q3 2026 results, covering the quarter ended November 1, showed sales up nearly 5% year-over-year. Small repair and refresh projects grew 2.1% compared to the prior year, driven by one simple fact: the housing market is sitting at a 16-year low. Homeowners aren't moving. They're staying put and fixing what they have, which means more trips to the hardware store, more weekend projects, and more money spent on paint, fixtures, and repair materials. As CNBC reported on November 17, McPhail told analysts he plans to lean into AI-driven selling tools and double down on serving professional contractors, the so-called "Pro" customers who spend far more per visit than the average weekend warrior.

That combination, an AI-focused CEO with a Pro-first strategy running the world's largest home improvement retailer, is worth paying attention to if you're the person who buys a box of deck screws and asks a floor associate which joist hanger you need.

## Who Is Richard McPhail and Why Does His Background Matter

McPhail spent years as Home Depot's CFO before this promotion. That's not a knock on him. It tells you exactly how he sees the business: through margin, efficiency, and return on investment. CFOs who become CEOs tend to make decisions that look good on a spreadsheet. For Home Depot, that likely means accelerating the investments that already show strong returns, specifically the Pro ecosystem (lumber yards, job-site delivery, dedicated Pro desks) and the AI tools the company has been rolling out to assist with product recommendations and project planning online.

Ann-Marie Campbell as President and COO is the counterweight here. Forty-one years at the company means she's worked every level of retail operations. She knows what a store looks like when it's well-run and when it isn't. That experience on the operations side could actually benefit in-store DIY shoppers more than McPhail's strategic priorities will, because someone has to make sure the shelves are stocked and the staff can answer a question about pipe fittings.

## What the Pro Focus Means for Regular DIY Shoppers

Home Depot has been building out its Pro customer program for years, and McPhail made clear to CNBC that this continues. Pro customers, meaning contractors, builders, and facilities managers, buy in bulk, return less, and don't need their hand held through a purchase. They're a better margin customer. So stores get organized around their needs: dedicated checkout lanes, job-site delivery, bulk pricing on fasteners and lumber.

Here's what that can mean for a DIY shopper on a Saturday afternoon. Floor staff get pulled toward Pro desks. Inventory priorities shift toward contractor-grade quantities and SKUs. The in-store experience, already leaning heavily on self-checkout and digital lookup kiosks, gets even more automated as AI tools replace what used to be a conversation with a knowledgeable associate.

That's not catastrophic, but it's worth knowing. If you need help picking the right underlayment for a floating floor or figuring out what size breaker your new range requires, the in-store assistance may become less reliable over time. The workaround: use the AI tools Home Depot is building. If McPhail invests in them well, they may actually be more accurate than a part-time floor associate who was hired last month.

## The Housing Slump Is Actually Good for Your Project Budget Right Now

A 16-year low in housing market activity creates a specific kind of market condition that homeowners doing projects can take advantage of. Contractors are more available. Lead times on trades like tile setters, painters, and finish carpenters are shorter than they were in 2021 or 2022. Material prices on some categories have stabilized after the post-pandemic spike.

Here's a rough look at how the current project environment compares to the peak renovation frenzy of a few years ago:

| Project Factor | 2021-2022 Peak | Late 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Contractor availability | Booked 3-6 months out | Often 2-4 weeks |
| Lumber pricing (framing, per board foot) | $1.50+ at peak | Closer to $0.55-0.65 |
| Permit wait times (major metro avg.) | 8-14 weeks | 4-8 weeks |
| DIY small project spending trend | Flat/declining | Up 2.1% YoY |

The numbers on contractor wait times and lumber vary by region, but the directional trend is real. If you've been putting off a bathroom refresh, a deck repair, or a [kitchen backsplash](/how-to-install-a-kitchen-backsplash-yourself/) because you couldn't get anyone to show up, right now is a better window than it was two years ago.

## When to DIY and When to Call a Pro, Given All This

The McPhail-led Home Depot is going to make it easier to buy materials and harder to get advice. That pushes more homeowners toward either confident DIY or hiring a professional, with less in-between hand-holding. So here's a clear-eyed breakdown of where that line should be.

**Do it yourself:** Paint, caulking, tile backsplash on a flat surface, installing a ceiling fan on an existing box, replacing a faucet, patching drywall up to about 6 inches, installing LVP flooring in a simple rectangular room.

**Call a licensed pro:** Anything involving your main electrical panel, any gas line work, structural changes including removing walls, foundation repairs, roofing beyond minor shingle replacement, and any plumbing that requires cutting into supply lines inside walls.

The projects in the middle, like installing a bathroom vanity with a shutoff valve replacement, or running a new 20-amp circuit to a kitchen outlet, are where your honest assessment of your skill level has to drive the call. If you've never touched that type of work before, the housing slowdown means a pro is both more available and more fairly priced than they were recently.

## What to Watch Going Forward

McPhail takes the chair November 23, 2026. The strategic shifts he telegraphed to CNBC won't show up overnight in your local store. They'll show up over the next 12 to 24 months in staffing models, store layout changes, and how much budget goes into the app versus into training floor associates. Watch how Home Depot's online project planning tools evolve. Watch whether the in-store Pro desk footprint expands at the expense of the general floor. And watch whether the 2.1% growth in small DIY projects continues or accelerates as the housing freeze holds.

For now, the store is still the store. McPhail's Q3 results were solid, the shelves are stocked, and if you need to fix something in your house this winter, you've got more reasons to get it done than to wait.
