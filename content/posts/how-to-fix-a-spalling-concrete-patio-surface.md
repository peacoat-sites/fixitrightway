Spalling concrete is fixable, and in most cases you don't need to tear anything out. The question is whether you're dealing with surface flaking that's a quarter-inch deep or less, or structural deterioration that's crumbling through the slab. That distinction decides everything: your repair method, your materials, and whether a DIY fix will actually hold.

Surface spalling on a patio usually shows up as shallow pitting, flaking "chips," or a peeling layer that looks almost papery. It's caused by freeze-thaw cycles forcing water out of the concrete, deicing salt damage, or a finishing defect called "overworking" where too much cream rises to the surface during the original pour and creates a weak top layer. All of those are repairable. What's not a good DIY candidate is a slab that's actively heaving, has cracks wider than a half-inch, or shows rebar rusting through the surface.

## How Bad Is It, Really?

Before you buy anything, spend ten minutes with a cold chisel and a hammer. Tap across the spalled areas and listen. A solid "thud" means the concrete beneath is sound. A hollow "tick" means delamination has spread under the surface, even if it looks intact. Any area that sounds hollow needs to come out before you patch over it.

Chip away everything that's loose, soft, or crumbling until you're down to solid concrete. This is not optional. A patch applied over weakened concrete will pop off within one or two freeze-thaw cycles, and you'll be back to square one.

Also check depth. Grab a pencil or a small screwdriver and measure the deepest pits. Under a quarter-inch, almost any surface resurfacer will work. Between a quarter-inch and three-quarters of an inch, you need a repair mortar or a polymer-modified topping mix. Deeper than that, especially if you can see aggregate that's been exposed for a while and looks gray and powdery, consider calling a concrete contractor to assess whether a full resurfacing or even a partial slab replacement makes more sense.

## Choosing the Right Product

This is where most homeowners go wrong. They grab a bag of generic concrete mix or vinyl patching compound because it's familiar. Those products can work, but they're not always the right tool.

Quikrete Concrete Resurfacer and similar products from Sakrete are genuinely good for patios with widespread shallow spalling. They're polymer-modified, bond well to properly prepared surfaces, and can be applied thin. For isolated pits, a vinyl or acrylic-modified repair mortar does the job without requiring you to resurface the entire slab.

One common misconception: "more is better" with patching compounds. Applying a thicker-than-recommended layer doesn't make the repair stronger. It often traps moisture and causes the patch to crack as it cures.

## The Repair Process, Step by Step

Preparation takes longer than the actual patching. Budget at least half your total time for it.

**1. Clear and clean the surface.** Remove all furniture, planters, and anything covering the slab. Sweep, then wash the entire patio with a pressure washer at 3,000 PSI or less (higher can damage sound concrete). If you see oil stains, treat them with a concrete degreaser before washing. Let the surface dry for 24 hours.

**2. Remove all failing material.** Use a cold chisel and 3-lb hammer (or an angle grinder with a diamond cup wheel for larger areas) to cut out every loose, flaking, or hollow-sounding section. The goal is to reach solid concrete on all sides. Undercut the edges slightly if you can, a slight backwards angle (not a feathered taper) gives the patch more mechanical grip.

**3. Vacuum and blow out the repair areas.** Dust and fine particles kill adhesion. A shop vac followed by compressed air is the right sequence.

**4. Pre-wet, but don't saturate.** Concrete repair products bond best to a surface that's saturated surface dry (SSD): damp to the touch, with no standing water or shiny wet spots. A dry surface pulls water out of the repair product too fast, weakening the cure. A wet surface prevents proper bonding.

**5. Apply a bonding agent if required.** Some repair mortars require a bonding slurry (a thin mix of the same product brushed into the repair area) before you apply the main patch. Read the product instructions. Skipping this step is one of the most common reasons patches fail within a year.

**6. Mix and apply.** Follow the water ratio on the bag exactly. Too much water weakens the finished product, every time. Work the material firmly into the repair area with a margin trowel or putty knife, pressing it into corners and against edges. For surface resurfacer applied across a large area, use a long-handled squeegee to spread it, then a broom finish or texture roller to match the surrounding surface.

**7. Cure it properly.** This is the step people always skip. Cover fresh repairs with plastic sheeting or damp burlap for at least 24 hours, ideally 72. Direct sun and low humidity pull moisture out of the repair before it gains strength. In temperatures below 50°F, don't apply most resurfacers at all, check the product's temperature range.

**8. Seal when cured.** A penetrating concrete sealer (silane-siloxane type) applied after the repair cures will protect both the new material and the surrounding slab from future water intrusion. This is especially important in freeze-thaw climates. Plan to reapply every 3 to 5 years.

## When to Stop and Call Someone

Don't proceed with DIY patching if:

- Cracks run through the full depth of the slab and shift slightly when you step on either side of them.
- The patio is settling unevenly (one section noticeably lower or higher than adjacent areas).
- You see rust stains radiating from subsurface cracks, which indicates corroding rebar.
- More than roughly 30 to 40 percent of the surface is failing. At that point, a full resurfacing or replacement is usually more economical than patching.

A concrete contractor can assess structural issues and quote you on options like slabjacking (pumping material beneath a sunken section to raise it) or full replacement. Get at least two quotes before deciding, the spread can be significant.

**Example:** Say you have a 200-square-foot patio with shallow spalling across about 60 square feet. At roughly 40 pounds of resurfacer per 50 square feet (a typical coverage rate for a thin application), you'd need around 50 to 60 pounds of product. Two 40-lb bags ($25 to $40 each) covers the material cost, plus maybe $20 for a bonding agent and $30 to $50 for a sealer. Total materials: $100 to $150. A contractor resurfacing the same patio would likely run $400 to $900 depending on prep work required and your region. The DIY math is favorable if the slab itself is structurally sound.
