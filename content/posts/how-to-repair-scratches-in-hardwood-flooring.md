Three years ago, a homeowner called me in a panic because her golden retriever had turned a 200-square-foot section of Brazilian cherry flooring into what looked like a topographic map. She wanted to know if she needed to refinish the whole floor. The answer was no, and it cost her about $23 in supplies to fix 90% of it herself. That's what I want to talk about today.

[Hardwood floor](/how-to-fix-a-squeaky-hardwood-floor-from-above/) scratches are one of those things that look catastrophic up close and completely invisible from six feet away, once you've addressed them correctly. But I've seen people spend $1,200 on professional refinishing jobs that weren't necessary, and I've also seen people use the wrong product and permanently cloud their finish. The difference usually comes down to understanding what kind of scratch you're actually dealing with before you reach for anything.

The single most important diagnostic step is figuring out whether the scratch is in the finish or in the wood itself. Drag your fingernail across it. If your nail catches, the wood fiber is damaged. If it glides right over, you're dealing with a surface scratch in the polyurethane or oil finish, and your life just got a lot easier.

## What You're Actually Looking At

[Hardwood floors](/cost-to-refinish-hardwood-floors-yourself-vs-hire/) typically fail in two ways: the finish scratches, or the wood underneath takes actual damage. Most pet scratches, chair leg drag marks, and dropped-key incidents are finish scratches. The wood fiber is intact. A good light source at a low angle (hold your phone flashlight about two inches off the floor and look toward the scratch) will tell you immediately. Finish scratches look shiny or hazy depending on how they caught the light. Wood damage looks fibrous, dull, and often has a different color because the raw wood underneath is exposed.

There's a third category most guides skip: dents. A dent isn't technically a scratch, but people treat them the same way and get confused when scratch repair products don't work. A dent is compressed wood fiber. You can sometimes raise it with a damp cloth and a clothes iron (low setting, never touch the iron directly to bare wood), but this only works reliably on solid hardwood, not engineered.

One more thing to identify: your floor's finish type. Oil-modified polyurethane, water-based polyurethane, hardwax oil, and aluminum oxide coatings all behave differently and require different products. Oil-based poly has an amber tint and a plastic-like feel. Water-based is clearer and slightly cooler to the touch. Hardwax oil floors are increasingly common on European-style white oak installations and have a matte, almost velvety look. If you're not sure, call your flooring installer or check the original product spec sheet if you have it. I can't stress this enough: using the wrong chemistry here creates a milky haze that's genuinely difficult to reverse.

## The Repair Options, From Easiest to Most Involved

Here's a real look at the options side by side, because I get asked constantly whether a $12 product is going to work as well as a $200 service call.

| Repair Method | Best For | DIY or Pro | Approximate Cost | Time to Complete |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Finish marker / touch-up pen | Fine surface scratches, single boards | DIY | $8-18 | 15 minutes |
| Wax stick (Minwax, Mohawk) | Shallow wood-fiber scratches | DIY | $6-14 | 20-30 minutes |
| Blended filler + color-matched putty | Deep scratches, gouges up to 1/4" | DIY or Pro | $15-40 in supplies | 1-2 hours |
| Spot refinishing (sand + recoat single board) | Localized deep damage | Pro recommended | $150-350 per area | Half day |
| Screen and recoat (whole floor) | Widespread fine scratches, dull finish | Pro | $0.75-1.50/sq ft | 1 day + dry time |
| Full sand and refinish | Deep damage, color change, warping | Pro | $3-8/sq ft | 2-4 days |

The wax sticks (Minwax makes a good one, about $7 at most hardware stores) genuinely work on surface-level wood scratches. You heat the stick slightly with a hair dryer, press it into the scratch, and scrape the excess flat with a plastic putty knife or an old credit card. The skill is in color matching, which is the part nobody warns you about.

## Color Matching Is Where Most People Get This Wrong

I'll be honest: I thought color matching was easy until I tried to fix a scratch on a 15-year-old red oak floor with a single wax stick and ended up with a repair that looked like someone had pressed a crayon into the floor. The problem is that hardwood darkens and shifts color over years of UV exposure and wear. A "red oak medium" stick from the store is calibrated to new red oak, not aged red oak.

The fix is to buy two or three adjacent shades and blend them. Mohawk sells a "Fil-Stik" set in a multipack (around $14 for a set of four) that lets you layer colors. Work in natural light if you can. Apply the darker shade first, then the lighter, then blend with your finger. The goal isn't a perfect match under a microscope; it's a match from standing height. You'll almost always get there with layering.

For finish scratches without wood damage, the Howard Products Feed-N-Wax is popular but honestly a little overrated for actual scratch repair. It conditions the wood and can fill very fine micro-scratches, but anything you can feel with your fingernail needs more than a conditioning oil. Bona's Hardwood Floor Cleaner with their Polish (roughly $18 for the combo) works better on fine finish scratches because it fills and levels the polyurethane layer slightly. That's the product I used on my own floors after we moved furniture during a kitchen renovation and left some real drag marks.

## When You're Looking at a Gouge, Not a Scratch

A gouge is deeper, wider, and often has splintered or lifted wood fiber around the edges. Before you do anything else, press down any lifted fibers with a drop of wood glue (Titebond II, not super glue) and a flat weight. Let it cure 24 hours. Skipping this step means you're filling over loose material, and the repair will pop out.

For the fill itself, two-part epoxy wood fillers like LiquidWood from Abatron or PC-Woody give you a much harder, more durable repair than soft wax. They're workable for about 20-30 minutes after mixing, which is more time than you think but less than you want if you're fussy. Once cured (4-6 hours for most brands), sand flush with 120-grit followed by 150-grit, then finish with a touch of matching stain if needed and a wipe of matching finish product.

Here's the worked example: a homeowner in Phoenix dropped a cast-iron skillet in the kitchen, put a gouge about 5/16" deep and 1.5" long in her hickory floor. We used PC-Woody, color-matched with two drops of Mixol #10 tint stirred into the mix before application, sanded flush after curing, and applied a single coat of Bona Traffic HD finish with a small foam brush. Standing result: nearly invisible. Total supplies: $31. Time: about two hours spread over two days for cure times.

## When to Call a Professional

There are situations where DIY repair genuinely doesn't make sense. If the scratches are widespread enough that you're looking at more than 15-20% of the floor's surface area, a screen-and-recoat job (where a pro lightly abrades the entire surface and applies fresh finish coats [without sanding](/how-to-repaint-kitchen-cabinets-without-sanding/) down to bare wood) will look dramatically better than spot repairs and costs significantly less than full refinishing. As of July 2026, screen-and-recoat typically runs $0.75-$1.50 per square foot in most markets, so a 400-square-foot living room runs $300-$600 installed.

Full sanding is a bigger deal: it removes the old finish down to bare wood, lets you change stain color if you want, and essentially resets the clock on the floor. It produces a lot of dust even with dustless equipment, takes 2-4 days including dry time, and requires you to be out of the space. It's genuinely worthwhile if the floor has deep damage, cupping that's resolved, or if you want a completely different look. Not worth it for a handful of scratches.

One scenario where I always recommend a pro: floors finished with aluminum oxide (common on prefinished engineered hardwood, Pergo, and similar products). The finish is incredibly hard and bonds to the wood differently. DIY touch-up products often won't adhere properly, and sanding it requires industrial equipment. Most flooring contractors have specific experience with these; a homeowner trying to spot-sand it usually creates a dull ring around the repair that's more visible than the original scratch.
