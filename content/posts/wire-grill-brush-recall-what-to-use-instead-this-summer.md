If you've got a wire grill brush hanging on a hook in your garage right now, you might be wondering whether it's one of the recalled ones. You're not alone. Millions of people are in the same spot this summer, and the timing couldn't be more inconvenient: the two biggest recalls of wire-bristle grill brushes in recent memory both dropped in the first quarter of 2026, right before grilling season hit full stride. Weber recalled over 3.2 million brushes on February 26, 2026, and Nexgrill followed on March 26, 2026 with a recall covering more than 10.2 million brushes. Combined, that's 13.4 million brushes that need to come out of circulation. If yours is affected, here's what to do about it, and what to reach for instead.

## How to Know If Your Brush Is Recalled

Start with the brand and where you bought it. The Nexgrill recall specifically covers brushes sold at Home Depot stores and online between 2015 and 2026, priced between $5 and $15. According to the [CPSC's March 26 recall notice](https://cpsc.gov/Recalls/2026/Nexgrill-Recalls-Over-10-2-Million-Metal-Wire-Bristle-Grill-Brushes-Due-to-Ingestion-Hazard-Sold-at-Home-Depot), the six affected model numbers are 530-0024, 530-0024G, 530-0034, 530-0039, 530-0041, and 530-0042. Check the packaging or the brush handle itself for that number. If you tossed the packaging years ago, check Home Depot's purchase history in your account if you bought it online, or contact Nexgrill directly.

The Weber recall is broader in terms of retailer spread. Those brushes were sold at Lowe's, Home Depot, Ace Hardware, Target, and Amazon from 2011 through 2026. If you've had a Weber-branded wire brush sitting around for a few years, assume it may be included and check Weber's claims portal to confirm.

Neither of these brushes looks obviously dangerous. That's the whole problem.

## Why This Hazard Is So Serious

Here's what I tell people when they ask if this is really a big deal: yes, genuinely. The CPSC received at least 68 reports of bristles detaching from Nexgrill brushes alone, including 5 cases where people needed medical treatment to remove bristles from their throats or digestive tracts. Those aren't minor incidents. A wire bristle is thin enough that it can stick to a grate, transfer to food, get swallowed without anyone noticing, and then cause a perforation or get lodged somewhere it shouldn't be.

CPSC Chair Peter Feldman, in his [February 26 statement on the Weber recall](https://www.cpsc.gov/About-CPSC/Chairman/Peter-A-Feldman/Statement/Chairman-Feldman-Statement-on-Weber-Metal-Wire-Bristle-Grill-Brush-Recall-Manufacturers-Must-Address-Known-Hazards-in-Metal-Wire-Bristle-Grill-Brushes), called it "a dangerous design defect that allows metal bristles to detach and be swallowed without detection" and urged consumers to switch to non-wire alternatives immediately. That language, coming from a regulatory chair, is unusually direct. They're not hedging.

The scarier part, from a practical standpoint: you won't taste a bristle. You won't necessarily feel it. Kids are especially at risk because they're not going to inspect a hot dog before eating it.

## How to Get Your Refund

Don't let this sit. Both companies are making it relatively straightforward to claim a refund, and the process takes maybe five minutes.

For Nexgrill, you can submit a claim at nexgrill.mktpoint.com/recall. Refunds are issued as gift cards in the amount you paid, which was $5 to $15 depending on the model. It's not a windfall, but it's your money back for a product you now need to stop using. For Weber, there's a separate claims process through Weber's website. In both cases, you'll need to destroy the brush and may be asked to provide a photo as confirmation.

Don't donate it, don't pass it along to a neighbor, and don't just set it aside thinking you'll deal with it later. Throw it away first, then file the claim with your phone.

## What to Actually Use Instead

This is the question most articles skim over, so let me be specific. There are several non-wire alternatives that work well and don't carry this risk.

Bristle-free grill brushes use stainless steel coil or spiral-spring designs instead of wire bristles, so there's nothing to shed. Brands like Grillart and Kona make well-reviewed versions in the $15 to $30 range, and they're widely available. They clean effectively on standard grates and hold up well through a season.

Grill stones, sometimes called pumice cleaning blocks, are another solid option. You drag them across a warm grate, and they scrub off carbon and grease without any shedding risk at all. They wear down over time but are inexpensive to replace, usually $8 to $12.

Wooden scrapers are worth considering if you have a grill with a consistent grate pattern. You press the scraper against the hot grate a few times when you first get it, and it forms grooves that match your specific grate. From there it cleans reasonably well. They're not quite as aggressive as a good coil brush, but for regular maintenance cleaning after each cook, they do the job.

As [HGTV noted in their March 31 coverage](https://www.hgtv.com/gardening/gardening-tips/metal-wire-bristle-grill-brush-recall), the CPSC recommends inspecting your grill grates carefully even after switching tools, just as a habit. I'd add: inspect the grate before you put food on it, not just after you clean it. Takes two seconds.

## The Broader Lesson Before Summer Gets Going

These recalls feel like they came out of nowhere, but wire bristle brushes have drawn concern from safety researchers for years. What changed in early 2026 is the regulatory pressure, the scale of the recalls, and the timing, which puts it squarely in your lap before the Fourth of July.

You might be wondering if you're overreacting by taking this seriously. You're not. A $10 to $25 replacement tool is one of the lower-stakes safety upgrades you'll make this year, and the downside of ignoring it is genuinely not worth it.

Check your model number, file for the refund you're owed, and pick up a coil brush or grill stone before your next cookout. That's really all there is to it.
