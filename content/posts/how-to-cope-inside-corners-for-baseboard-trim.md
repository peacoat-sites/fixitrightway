Most guides on coping [inside corners](/how-to-cut-crown-molding-inside-corners/) show you a perfect cut on perfect pine and call it a day. What they skip: what to do when the wall isn't 90 degrees, what coping actually looks like on the second piece of trim (not the first), and why your coped joint opens up six months later even when you nailed it.

Let's fix that.

Coping is the professional's answer to inside corners. The beginner move is mitering both pieces at 45 degrees and hoping the walls are square. They almost never are. A coped joint, where one piece of baseboard runs flat into the corner and the second piece is cut to ride over its face, adjusts for out-of-square walls, doesn't gap when wood moves seasonally, and looks tighter ten years out than any miter ever will.

## Why One Piece Goes Straight

Here's what I thought for the first couple of years I was doing trim work: both pieces at an inside corner need to be fancy cuts. Wrong. The first piece, the one running into the corner on the short wall or left side by convention, gets cut dead square and butted straight into the drywall. It just sits there, flat and boring. The magic happens on the second piece.

That first piece is your template. Its profile, whatever ogee or colonial or ranch-style shape the baseboard has, is what the cope cut traces. When you run the second piece across it, the profile of piece one should disappear behind piece two. That's the whole trick.

A mistake I see constantly on job sites: someone copes both pieces. The result looks like two people trying to shake hands wrong.

## The Actual Cut

Get a 10-inch piece of scrap from your baseboard stock. Start with this before you touch your good stock. You'll ruin at least one before you find your rhythm, and scrap is free.

**Step 1: Make a 45-degree outside miter cut on the end you're coping.** Run the baseboard face-up on your miter saw, set to 45 degrees, and cut the end that will go into the corner. This reveals the profile of the molding as a crisp line. That line is your coping line.

**Step 2: Cut along that profile with a coping saw.** Hold the coping saw at roughly a 15-degree back-bevel (blade angling away from the face). You want only the very front edge of the cope to make contact with piece one, not the whole thickness. If you cut straight through at 90 degrees, the joint will bind and gap. The back-bevel is what makes it close tight.

Go slow around the curves. The coping saw wants to wander. Let the blade do the work; don't force it. On built-up or complex profiles, a Dremel or oscillating multi-tool with a fine wood blade (Bosch's AIZ 32 AB fine wood blade runs about $12) can handle the tight curves better than a coping saw.

**Step 3: Test fit before you nail anything.** Hold the coped piece up against piece one. Look at it from both sides. The face should close tight. Minor high spots? A half-round file or a coarse sanding block clears them in under a minute. I keep a Nicholson 6-inch half-round bastard file in my apron specifically for this.

**Step 4: Adjust for wall angle.** If the cope closes at the top but gaps at the bottom (or vice versa), your wall corner isn't 90 degrees. Shave the back of the cope slightly to open up the angle, or tilt the molding on your miter saw bed a degree or two when you make the 45-degree cut. This takes feel more than formula. You'll know when you have it.

**Step 5: Nail and caulk.** Nail into studs, pre-drill near ends to avoid splits, and run a thin bead of paintable latex caulk along the cope line before painting. The caulk fills any hairline gap and becomes invisible under paint. Don't skip this step even on a perfect cope.

## Tools and What They Actually Cost

You don't need much. A coping saw costs $12-$28 (Stanley FatMax is about $18 and holds up fine). A quality oscillating multi-tool like the Fein Multimaster runs $180-$220 and is overkill for one room but indispensable if you're doing a whole house. For most homeowners doing one project, the coping saw is plenty.

| Tool | Cost (August 2026) | Best For | Skip If |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coping saw (Stanley FatMax) | $18 | One-room DIY, simple profiles | You're doing 10+ rooms |
| Oscillating multi-tool (Ridgid JobMax) | $89 | Faster cuts, complex profiles | Budget is tight |
| Oscillating multi-tool (Fein Multimaster) | $209 | Pro-grade, daily use | This is your only trim job |
| Half-round file (Nicholson 6") | $11 | Fine-tuning the fit | Never skip this |
| Miter saw (existing) | $0 extra | Setting up the cope cut | Hand miter box works too |

The miter saw you already have for squaring pieces works for setting up the 45-degree reveal. You don't need a compound miter saw; a simple 10-inch miter saw handles baseboards fine.

## Where Cope Joints Fail (And Why)

Six months after install, you come back and the joint has opened up a hair. Two causes, almost always.

First: wood movement. Paint-grade MDF baseboard moves almost zero with humidity. Real pine or poplar can shift noticeably, especially in climates with dry winters. In my experience, solid wood baseboards in non-climate-controlled spaces like garages or three-season rooms need a slightly tighter cope than you think, because they'll open up come January.

Second: the back-bevel wasn't steep enough. If the cope sits on its full thickness rather than its front edge, any compression in the wall or slight nail movement pushes the joint open. The fix after the fact is to pull the piece, deepen the back-bevel with a file, and re-nail. Or just caulk it if it's under 3/16".

Worked examples from actual projects:

A 14x18 bedroom remodel with standard 3.5-inch colonial baseboard: first cope cut took 9 minutes and fit acceptably; second took 3 minutes and fit tight. Paint and caulk made both invisible. Total baseboard time for the room was 4 hours including measuring, cutting, and nailing.

A kitchen with out-of-square corners (worst was 86 degrees off square, confirmed with a digital angle finder): mitering would have left a visible gap on both sides. Coped joints required a back-bevel adjustment on the miter saw bed of 2 degrees and fit closed on first test-fit.

A basement finish with finger-jointed pine baseboard in an unconditioned space: joint opened approximately 1/8" over the first winter. Re-coped with a steeper back-bevel and added a second nail closer to the corner. Joint has stayed tight through two more seasons.

## DIY vs. Hiring It Out

Coping is learnable in an afternoon. It's also the single trim skill that separates finish carpenters who know what they're doing from those who are guessing. If you're comfortable with a miter saw and have patience for test fits, do it yourself.

Hiring a finish carpenter (as of August 2026) runs $65-$95 per hour in most metro markets. A typical 12x15 room with baseboard and door casings takes a pro about 3-4 hours: $195-$380 labor, plus $130-$200 in materials for paint-grade MDF baseboard at $1.40-$1.80 per linear foot. DIY on the same room cuts the labor cost to zero and adds maybe 6-8 hours of your time including two trips to the lumber yard.

The math is straightforward. The skill ceiling isn't particularly high. Unless you have one room to do and genuinely no interest in learning it, cope it yourself.
