You probably noticed the damaged plank weeks ago and have been stepping around it ever since. Maybe it's a bubble from a slow leak under the dishwasher, or a crack from something heavy getting dropped, or just that one high-traffic spot near the back door that finally gave up. Whatever caused it, here's what most people don't realize: replacing a single laminate plank is genuinely doable on a weekend afternoon, and it doesn't require tearing up your whole floor.

That said, there are a few ways to make this harder than it needs to be.

## Before You Touch Anything: Figure Out What You're Actually Working With

Laminate floors click together using a tongue-and-groove locking system. The specific lock profile matters more than most DIY articles admit, because it determines exactly how you'll get a replacement plank in and out.

Most floors installed in the last 15 years use either a Unilin-style click lock or an Allure/FasTak-style peel-and-stick (which is actually vinyl, not laminate, but people mix them up constantly). For laminate specifically, you're almost certainly dealing with click-lock planks. Within that category there's a big difference between "fold-down" locking and "side-snap" locking. Fold-down means you angle the short end up at roughly 45 degrees and fold it down to lock. Side-snap means planks slide horizontally into each other. Knowing which one you have changes your whole approach.

Here's how to figure it out without destroying anything: pull up the floor vent cover nearest the damaged area and look at the edge of the planks from that angle. You can usually see the profile clearly. If you still can't tell, search the brand name plus "installation instructions" on Google. Pergo, Shaw, Armstrong, Mohawk, they all have PDFs floating around.

Also: save a plank before you do anything else. Check your garage, basement, or that weird shelf in your laundry room. A lot of builders leave a box of extras after install. If you find them, your job just got much easier. If you don't, photograph the back of the damaged plank (the brand stamp and thickness, usually 8mm, 10mm, or 12mm) and bring it to a flooring store. Getting an exact match on color and texture is the hard part of this job. Getting the wrong locking profile is a problem you'll feel immediately.

## Your Two Real Options (and When Each Makes Sense)

When a single damaged plank happens, you have two approaches. The right one depends entirely on where that plank is.

**Option 1: Work from the wall and disassemble row by row.**

If your damaged plank is within a few rows of a wall, this is the cleaner method. You pull the baseboard trim off that wall (a flat bar and some patience), then you click planks apart row by row until you reach the bad one. Swap it, reassemble in reverse order. This takes longer, but it's clean, low-risk, and the floor looks exactly like it did before. The downside is that "a few rows" can still mean 45 minutes of careful work, and if your baseboard is glued down rather than nailed, you're adding a whole other problem.

**Option 2: Cut out the damaged plank in place.**

If the bad plank is in the middle of the room, you're not pulling up 12 rows of flooring to get there. Instead, you cut the damaged plank out and use a technique called a "blind install" to get the replacement in. This is the trickier method, but it's what professionals do in this situation.

## The Cut-and-Replace Method, Step by Step

**What you'll need:** a circular saw or oscillating multi-tool (a Fein MultiMaster or the DeWalt DCS356 both work great), a utility knife, a flat pry bar, a pull bar, a tapping block, construction adhesive like Loctite PL Premium, painter's tape, and the replacement plank.

Set your circular saw blade depth to just barely through the plank thickness. This is non-negotiable. You do not want to cut into the subfloor. Most laminate is 8-12mm, so set your blade to about 10-12mm. Make a test cut in a scrap piece first if you're not sure.

1. Draw a cut line about half an inch in from each long edge of the damaged plank, parallel to the length. Score those lines with a utility knife first. Then carefully make your circular saw cuts along those lines, stopping short of the end joints by about an inch on each side.

2. Use the oscillating tool to finish those end cuts near the joints. An oscillating tool here is better than a saw because you can work in tight quarters without blowing past your line.

3. You now have a strip you can remove from the center. Pry it up, then use your flat bar to carefully work out the two remaining side pieces. Go slow on the tongue-and-groove edges so you don't damage the neighboring planks.

4. Examine what's underneath. If there's moisture damage or the underlayment is destroyed, fix that now. A small piece of replacement underlayment (most flooring stores sell it by the foot) and a few minutes to dry is way better than trapping a problem under a new plank.

5. Modify the replacement plank: on the bottom of the long-side groove (the side that would snap down into the existing floor), use a utility knife or chisel to remove the lower lip. You're creating what's essentially a slip-tongue on that side. This is the key step that makes the blind install possible. Don't skip it.

6. Apply a thin bead of Loctite PL Premium or similar construction adhesive along the top of the groove on the surrounding planks, the edges that will mate with your new plank. Don't go heavy. A thin, even bead is all you need.

7. Angle the new plank's tongue side into the groove of one long edge. Carefully lower it flat, then tap the remaining sides in with a tapping block and rubber mallet. The modified groove side should slide in cleanly. Use painter's tape across the seams to hold everything tight while the adhesive cures, usually 24 hours.

The seam will be almost invisible if your replacement plank matches well. The adhesive makes this repair permanent rather than floating, which is fine for a single plank.

## The Part Everyone Underestimates

Matching the plank.

I can't tell you how many times I've seen a technically perfect repair ruined by a plank that's even slightly off. The color isn't just about species or stain, it's about the embossing texture, the sheen level, and the wear layer pattern. A plank from the same brand but a different production run can look noticeably different under certain light.

If you have leftover planks from the original install, use them. Full stop.

If you don't, take the damaged plank (or a clear, well-lit photo of it alongside a ruler) to a specialty flooring retailer, not a big box store. Stores like LL Flooring sometimes have discontinued matches or can order from manufacturer archives. Independent flooring dealers are even better because they'll actually dig through samples with you rather than pointing at an aisle.

If you genuinely can't find a match and the plank is in a visible area, steal a plank from inside a closet or under the refrigerator and use that for the repair. Then replace the less-visible plank with whatever close match you can find. It's a contractor trick I use more than I'd like to admit.

## When to Call a Pro

Most single-plank repairs don't require a pro. But a few situations warrant it.

If the damage came from water and you're not sure how far it spread, you need to know before you close that floor back up. Water under laminate is a mold situation waiting to happen. A moisture meter (you can rent one from Home Depot for about $30 a day, or buy a decent one like the General Tools MMD7NP for around $25) will tell you whether the subfloor is actually dry.

If the subfloor itself is damaged, soft, or bouncy, that's a structural repair before it's a flooring repair. Laminate over a compromised subfloor will sound hollow, shift, and fail again.

And if your floor was installed as a glue-down (some older laminate and most engineered hardwood was), the whole approach changes. Glue-down plank removal requires a floor scraper and considerably more time. That one I'd price out with a professional first, because labor on glue-down removal is legitimately messy.

One more thing: the repair you do this weekend doesn't have to be perfect forever. If you get it 90% right, that's a floor that looks good and performs well. Laminate is forgiving in a lot of ways, and a solid repair with decent technique will hold up for years. Don't let the pursuit of a perfect match or a flawless seam keep you from actually fixing the thing.
