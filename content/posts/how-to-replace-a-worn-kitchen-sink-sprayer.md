The sprayer head snapped off in my hand on a Tuesday morning, mid-rinse, with a sink full of greasy pans. Not a dramatic failure. Just the cheap plastic collar cracked, the head dangled from the hose, and suddenly I had a slow drip I couldn't ignore. Happens all the time. In fact, kitchen sink sprayers are one of the highest-failure points in any residential kitchen, mostly because people use them constantly but never think about them until something breaks.

The good news: replacing a sprayer is genuinely one of the easiest plumbing repairs you can do yourself. No soldering. No special licenses. I'd put it at about 30 minutes for a first-timer, less if you've done it once before. The less-good news is that there are a few ways to buy the wrong part or create a worse problem than you started with, and I've watched homeowners do exactly that.

What most people don't realize is that the sprayer hose and the sprayer head are often sold separately, and whether you need to replace both depends on where the failure actually is. Worth figuring that out before you order anything.

## What You're Actually Dealing With

The kitchen sprayer system has three parts that can fail: the head (the handpiece you grip), the hose (the braided or plastic line that connects it), and the diverter valve (a small internal part inside your main faucet body that redirects water when you press the sprayer trigger). Most people assume the head is the problem because that's what they're holding when it stops working. Sometimes they're right. Sometimes they're not.

Here's a quick diagnostic: If the sprayer has no pressure or very weak flow, squeeze the trigger and check whether the faucet flow drops. If it does, the diverter is probably still working and the problem is in the head or hose. If the faucet flow doesn't change at all when you squeeze the trigger, the diverter has likely failed. That's a different fix, and I'll cover it briefly below.

If the head is cracked, leaking at the collar, or just spraying in all directions because the restrictor inside has worn out, you're looking at a $10–$22 head replacement from a big-box store. If the hose itself is kinked beyond recovery, leaking from the body, or has that chalky brittle texture that tells you the plastic has degraded, replace the whole hose-and-head set. Sets run $18–$55 depending on finish and brand.

## The Replacement Process

Turn off the hot and cold supply valves under the sink first. I can't tell you how many times I've watched someone skip this step because they thought they could work fast enough. You can't. Just turn them off.

With the valves closed, turn on the faucet to bleed the remaining pressure. Then get under the sink.

The sprayer hose connects to the faucet body at the top, typically with a snap-in or threaded coupling that you access from below. There's usually a small weight clipped onto the hose (a metal ring or block) that helps the hose retract. Pull that off first and set it aside. You'll reuse it.

The coupling: some snap off with a firm pull after you compress a plastic tab. Others have a hex nut you loosen with a basin wrench or adjustable pliers. This is where people get stuck, because the space is tight and your hands are in an awkward position. I've found that lying on your back with a headlamp, rather than crouching, gives you a dramatically better angle. The connection is almost always 3/8" female thread or a proprietary quick-connect, and the replacement package will tell you which it is.

Once the hose is disconnected from the faucet body, pull it up through the sprayer hole in the sink deck. The old head unscrews from the hose end (counterclockwise). That's it for removal.

Reverse for installation: thread the new hose down through the sprayer hole, connect it to the faucet coupling (hand-tight plus a quarter turn, not gorilla-tight), clip the weight back on about 6 inches down from the coupling, and attach the new head. Turn the supply valves back on slowly. Test for leaks at both connection points.

One thing only someone who's done this knows: when you reattach the hose to the faucet coupling, there's often a small rubber O-ring that comes with the replacement hose. Don't lose it. And check the old connection for a deteriorated O-ring before you reassemble, because that tiny ring is frequently the actual source of the drip that started the whole problem.

## The Diverter Situation

If your diagnostic showed that the faucet flow doesn't drop when you press the trigger, you've got a diverter problem. The diverter is a small brass or plastic valve inside the faucet body, usually directly behind the spout, and mineral buildup is the most common reason it stops redirecting water properly.

Before you buy a replacement: try cleaning it first. Remove the faucet spout (usually a set screw or a clip at the base), pull out the diverter (it looks like a small cylindrical cartridge with a rubber seat), and soak it in white vinegar for an hour. I've done this myself on a Delta 470 and gotten another two years out of a diverter that seemed totally dead. Sometimes the mineral scale is just thick enough to prevent full seating.

If that doesn't work, bring the old diverter to a plumbing supply house or photograph it clearly for an online order. This is one case where I'd say go to an actual plumbing supply store rather than the big-box, because they can identify the part on sight and save you two return trips.

A reader named Michelle from Minneapolis emailed me last spring about exactly this situation: no sprayer pressure, replaced the head twice, still weak flow. Scenario: assumed the heads were defective. Action taken: pulled the diverter, found it was coated in scale, soaked it in vinegar overnight, reinstalled. Result: full pressure restored, total cost $0 and about 45 minutes.

## When to Call a Pro

Honestly? Almost never for a sprayer replacement alone. If the supply valves under your sink won't close fully (they spin freely without stopping flow), that's a separate issue that needs attention before you do any of this, and that one I'd have a plumber handle. Faulty shutoff valves are a water-damage risk.

If you open up the faucet body to get at the diverter and find significant corrosion or the body is cracked, stop and get a plumber's opinion. Forcing a corroded fitting is how small jobs become expensive ones.

For the straightforward sprayer swap, though: an actual plumber visit for this repair, if you called one, would run you $95–$175 for the service call plus parts, for a job that costs $20 in parts and half an hour of your time. I've seen people spend [that money](/why-that-money-saving-diy-project-could-void-your-insurance/) because they were intimidated. Don't be.
