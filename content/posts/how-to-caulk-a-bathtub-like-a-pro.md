If you've been staring at that cracked, blackened caulk line around your bathtub for weeks now, telling yourself you'll deal with it this weekend, you're in good company. It's one of the most common things I hear from homeowners: "I know I need to do it, I just don't know how to make it look right." And that second part matters, because a bad caulk job doesn't just look terrible. It lets water work its way behind the tub surround, into the subfloor, and sometimes down into the ceiling below. I've seen a $12 tube of caulk turn into a $4,000 water damage repair because someone put new caulk over old caulk and called it done. This is a job worth doing correctly the first time.

## What You'll Need (and What's Worth Spending Money On)

You don't need a lot of tools, but a few quality choices will make a significant difference in your results.

For materials, you'll want:

- **100% silicone caulk or a siliconized latex (also called "kitchen and bath" caulk):** Pure silicone lasts longer and resists mold better, but it's harder to work with and can only be cleaned up with mineral spirits. Siliconized latex is easier to tool and cleans up with water. Either works well for a tub surround. Avoid plain painter's caulk, which isn't designed for wet environments and will fail within months.
- **A caulk gun:** A standard $8 dripless gun is fine. The "dripless" feature means the plunger releases pressure when you stop squeezing, so caulk doesn't keep oozing out.
- **Caulk removal tool:** A plastic oscillating-blade scraper or a dedicated caulk remover tool runs about $5 to $10. Don't skip this.
- **Rubbing alcohol or acetone:** For cleaning the surface before you apply new caulk.
- **Painter's tape:** The secret weapon most tutorials skip, and the single biggest factor in a clean-looking job.
- **A small spray bottle with soapy water:** Mix a few drops of dish soap into water. This helps you smooth the caulk bead without it sticking to your finger.
- **Paper towels and a wet rag:** You'll want them close.

Total cost for supplies: roughly $20 to $35, assuming you buy a decent tube of caulk ($8 to $12 for a name brand like GE or DAP) and a caulk gun if you don't have one.

## Why Removing Old Caulk Is Non-Negotiable

Here's what I tell people who want to skip straight to applying new caulk over the old stuff: don't. It never works. New caulk doesn't bond properly to old caulk, especially silicone over silicone. Within a season, you'll have peeling, bubbling, and the same mold problem you started with, plus now you have two layers to deal with.

Removal is the step that takes the most time and the most patience. Plan for 30 to 60 minutes just for this part.

Start by scoring along both edges of the old caulk with a utility knife. You're cutting the bond between the caulk and the tile or tub surface, not trying to dig everything out at once. Use your caulk removal tool to peel and scrape. Most of it will come out in strips if the scoring was clean. For stubborn residue, a plastic scraper or your fingernail can get into crevices without scratching the tub surface.

Once the bulk is out, apply a caulk remover gel like Motsenbocker's Lift Off or 3M Caulk Remover to any remaining film. Let it sit for the time specified on the label, usually 2 to 4 hours, then wipe clean. This step matters most with silicone, which leaves an invisible oily residue that prevents new caulk from adhering properly.

Finish by wiping the entire joint with rubbing alcohol. Let it dry completely. If you see any pink or black mold staining on the tub or tile, spray it with a bleach solution (one part bleach, four parts water) and let it sit for 10 minutes before rinsing. Don't caulk over mold. You'll trap the spores and they'll spread beneath the new caulk.

## The Technique That Separates Good Jobs From Great Ones

Once your surface is clean and bone dry (and I mean dry, wait at least two hours after any cleaning), you're ready to tape and caulk.

Apply painter's tape in two parallel lines: one on the tub surface and one on the tile, each about 1/8 inch back from the joint. That gap between the tape lines is where your caulk will go, and it's what creates those crisp, straight edges that make a professional job look so clean. Without tape, even experienced people get a wobbly, uneven line.

Now, load your caulk tube into the gun. Cut the nozzle at about a 45-degree angle, and cut the opening small, roughly 3/16 to 1/4 inch. Smaller is better. A bead that's too big is hard to control and wastes material.

**Step-by-step application:**

1. Hold the gun at a 45-degree angle to the joint, with the angled tip pointing in the direction you're moving.
2. Apply steady, even pressure and move at a consistent speed. Think of it like icing a cake. Slow and steady wins.
3. Run the entire length of one section in a single pass if possible. Stopping and starting creates lumps.
4. Immediately spray the bead lightly with your soapy water solution.
5. Smooth the bead with a wet fingertip, one continuous stroke from end to end. Don't go back and forth. The soap keeps the caulk from sticking to your skin.
6. Pull the painter's tape off right away, before the caulk skins over. Pull it back at a 45-degree angle, slow and steady.
7. If the edges need a tiny touch-up, do it now with a damp fingertip before the caulk begins to cure.

Let the caulk cure fully before exposing it to water. Check the label. Most silicone caulks say 24 hours for a light rinse, but 72 hours for full water exposure. Siliconized latex usually cures faster. Don't rush this. Caulk that gets wet before it cures will pull away from the surface and you'll be starting over.

## Silicone vs. Siliconized Latex: Which Should You Actually Use?

This question comes up constantly, and the honest answer is that both work well if you apply them correctly. Here's a direct comparison:

| Feature | 100% Silicone | Siliconized Latex |
|---|---|---|
| Durability | 15 to 20+ years | 5 to 10 years |
| Mold resistance | Excellent | Good (some have antimicrobial additives) |
| Ease of application | More difficult, less forgiving | Easier to tool and smooth |
| Cleanup | Mineral spirits only | Soap and water |
| Paintability | No | Yes |
| Cost per tube | $8 to $14 | $6 to $10 |

In my experience, for a standard tub-tile junction, siliconized latex ("kitchen and bath" caulk) is the better choice for most homeowners. It's forgiving if this is your first time, it cleans up easily, and a good product like DAP 3.0 or GE Advanced will last years. If you want the most durable option available and you're comfortable with the less forgiving application process, go with 100% silicone. Just make sure the surface is spotless before applying it, because silicone will not adhere to any remaining residue.

## The Mistakes That Send People Back to Square One

I've been called in to fix more re-caulking jobs gone wrong than I can count. Here are the ones I see most often.

**Caulking over old caulk.** Already covered this, but it bears repeating. New over old doesn't work. Full removal is mandatory.

**Not filling the tub before caulking.** This is the mistake I see most often, even from people who otherwise do everything right. When a tub is empty, the joint is at its widest. When you fill the tub with water and step in, the weight compresses the floor and pulls the tub away from the wall, stretching that joint. If you caulked it empty, the caulk cracks the first time someone takes a bath. Fill the tub with water before you caulk, and leave it full until the caulk is fully cured.

**Using the wrong caulk.** Painter's caulk in a wet area, grout in a movement joint, or general-purpose caulk where kitchen-and-bath is needed. Read the label and buy the right product for the location.

**Applying caulk in a cold bathroom.** Most caulks need to be applied above 40 degrees Fahrenheit and perform best between 65 and 80 degrees. A cold tube of caulk is stiff and won't tool smoothly. If your bathroom is cool, let the tube sit in a warm room for an hour before you start.

**Skipping the drying time.** A caulk joint that gets wet before it cures will fail. This is not the project to start the night before you have houseguests.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does caulking a bathtub take, start to finish?

Budget a full day for the project, though most of that is waiting. Active work time for removal is 30 to 60 minutes. Cleaning and prep is another 30 minutes plus drying time. Actual caulk application is 20 to 30 minutes. Then you need 24 to 72 hours of cure time before the tub is back in use. If you try to rush any of those steps, especially removal or cure time, the job will fail faster than you'd expect.

### Can I caulk over mold, or do I need to treat it first?

You need to treat it first. Caulking over mold traps moisture and spores between the caulk and the substrate, where they'll continue to grow. Clean with a bleach solution, let it dry completely, and make sure there's no remaining discoloration before you apply new caulk. If the mold has penetrated into drywall or the tub surround material itself, you may need to cut out and replace that section before recaulking.

### My caulk keeps cracking after a few months. What am I doing wrong?

The two most common causes are caulking the tub while it's empty and using the wrong product. Fill the tub with water before caulking so the joint is at its widest point during application. Also double-check that you're using a flexible, waterproof bath caulk rather than grout, painter's caulk, or an all-purpose product. Finally, make sure the surface was completely clean and dry when you applied it. Any moisture or residue breaks the adhesive bond.

### Should I use white caulk or clear caulk for a white tub?

White caulk tends to look better long-term on white tubs and tile. Clear caulk can yellow with age and often shows soap scum more visibly. That said, if your grout lines are off-white or a specific color, clear can sometimes blend better than a stark white that doesn't match. Hold both up to your tile before you buy.

### When should I call a professional instead of doing this myself?

Caulking itself is a legitimate DIY job. Call a professional if: you find soft or spongy drywall behind the tub when you're removing old caulk (that's water damage that needs to be assessed), if there's active mold growth that extends beyond the surface, if the tub itself is pulling away from the wall and moving when you push on it (structural problem, not a caulk problem), or if you have a custom tile installation with unusual joint configurations. Those situations need more than a tube of caulk to fix properly.
