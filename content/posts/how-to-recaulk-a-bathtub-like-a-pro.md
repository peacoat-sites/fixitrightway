The old caulk around your tub doesn't fail all at once. It starts with one small section that looks a little gray, a little soft. You peel at it, and suddenly you've got a six-inch gap between the tub and the wall. I've watched homeowners spend $400 on a plumber to investigate what they thought was a mysterious leak, only to trace it back to exactly that: a failed caulk joint they'd been ignoring for two years.

Recaulking a bathtub is one of those jobs that genuinely belongs in the DIY column. It costs about $20 in materials, takes a Saturday morning, and -- done right -- lasts five to ten years. The problem is that most people do it wrong, and bad caulk work fails in six months, looks terrible, and convinces them it's harder than it is. It's not hard. It just has a specific process that most YouTube videos skip over.

---

## The First Step Most People Completely Skip

Before you buy a single tube of caulk, get all the old stuff out. Every last bit of it. I cannot tell you how many recaulk jobs I've seen fail because someone applied fresh caulk over old caulk. New caulk bonds to the surface. If the surface is old, failing caulk, you've just built your new seal on top of a problem.

For most tubs, you'll use a combination of a rigid plastic caulk removal tool (Dap makes a good set for around $7 at Home Depot) and a utility knife. Score along both edges of the old caulk bead with your knife, then work the plastic tool underneath and drag it along the joint. Silicone caulk especially has a way of leaving thin, translucent residue that you'll miss if you're not looking for it.

After the bulk is out, hit the joint with a rag soaked in isopropyl alcohol or a product called Lift Off Caulk Remover (about $8). Don't use acetone on acrylic or fiberglass tubs -- it can cloud the surface. Let everything dry completely, and I mean completely. If you're recaulking a shower or tub surround that gets used daily, do the prep work one evening and wait until the next morning to apply the new caulk. Moisture trapped under caulk is what causes mold to grow behind the bead. That grayish-black staining people think is surface mold is usually mold growing on the wall behind the caulk itself.

## Picking the Right Caulk (and Getting It Wrong Is Expensive)

Here's where I'll commit to an opinion: for tub and shower applications, use 100% silicone caulk. Not latex, not "paintable caulk," not "kitchen and bath" blends that are latex-based. Pure silicone.

Latex caulk is easier to work with because it's water-soluble while wet, so it's forgiving on application and you can smooth it with a damp finger. But in a wet area that cycles between soaking wet and bone dry, latex breaks down. It shrinks. It gets that cracked, chalky look. I've seen latex caulk in a tub surround fail in under a year. Silicone stays flexible, bonds tenaciously to non-porous surfaces like porcelain, acrylic, and ceramic tile, and holds up for years in high-moisture environments.

The catch is that silicone is unforgiving to apply and a pain to clean up. You're working with mineral spirits or denatured alcohol, and the working time before it starts skinning over is maybe 15 minutes, sometimes less in a warm room. So you do smaller sections, work deliberately, and have your smoothing tools ready.

My recommendation is GE Sealants Supreme Kitchen & Bath Silicone (white or clear, depending on your tub) or DAP Silicone Max. Both are available at big box stores for $10-13 a tube. One tube is almost always enough for a single tub. For a tub/shower combo with tile walls going six feet up, buy two.

One exception worth knowing: if you've got a colored or non-white grout situation and need something that matches, you may end up using a siliconized latex hybrid like Custom Building Products Polyblend Caulk. The color matching is excellent and the durability is decent. It won't last as long as pure silicone, but if you're trying to match a specific grout color, it's your best option.

## The Actual Application Process

Fill the tub with water before you caulk. I know that sounds backward.

Bathtubs flex under load -- even cast iron ones move slightly. An acrylic or fiberglass tub can drop a full 1/8 inch when filled with water and a person. If you caulk with an empty tub, the joint will stretch when the tub is in use, and the caulk will pull away from one surface or the other. Fill the tub with water, let it sit while you work, and drain it only after the caulk has cured. Leave the water in for the full 24-48 hour cure time if you can.

Cut the caulk tube tip at a 45-degree angle. The hole size matters. For a tub joint, you want an opening of about 3/16 inch -- most people cut too big and end up with a thick, sloppy bead that's hard to tool. Pierce the inner foil seal with the wire on your caulk gun or a long nail.

Tape both sides of the joint with painter's tape if you want clean lines. This is optional, but I'd recommend it for first-timers. Run the tape parallel to the joint about 1/8 inch back from the gap, so you're leaving a channel for the caulk. Press it down firmly.

Now apply. Hold your gun at roughly a 45-degree angle and pull it along the joint steadily. Don't go too slow (you'll get a fat, uneven bead) or too fast (you'll get a thin bead with gaps). Aim to fill the joint in one continuous pass. Work in three to four foot sections, then tool it immediately.

For tooling, wet your finger with isopropyl alcohol (not water -- silicone is hydrophobic and water will make it bead instead of tool smooth). Press your fingertip into the caulk and draw it along the joint with steady pressure. You're trying to push the caulk into the joint while creating a slightly concave surface. One pass, don't go back and fiddle. Remove your tape while the caulk is still wet. Pull it at a 45-degree angle back over itself -- not straight up. This keeps the edge clean.

Let it cure. Silicone needs 24 hours before light water exposure and a full 72 hours before normal shower use. Some products claim 24 hours is enough, and technically it might be, but I always wait the full three days if the schedule allows.

## What Goes Wrong (And How to Avoid It)

Mold behind the new bead, within weeks. Almost always means moisture was trapped during application -- either the surface wasn't dry, or the caulk was applied over residue that held moisture. Start over, prep better.

Caulk that peels away cleanly in one strip after a few months. That's an adhesion failure, and it usually comes from a dirty or contaminated surface. Soap film, body oils, even residue from cleaning products can prevent silicone from bonding. Alcohol wipe before you caulk, every time.

Bubbles or gaps in the bead. Happens when you're moving too fast or when the caulk tube was stored in a cold environment and the product is too thick to flow evenly. Let the tube sit at room temperature for an hour before you use it in winter.

A bead that cracks within a year. On a brand-new tub install, this is often because the tub is still settling slightly. On an older tub, it can be structural movement in the floor or subfloor. If you're recaulking the same joint for the third time, that's worth investigating before you reach for another tube.

## FAQ

### How long does recaulking a bathtub actually take?

The prep work (removing old caulk and cleaning) takes about an hour for an average tub. Application is 20-30 minutes. Then you're waiting 24-72 hours for cure time. Budget a full morning for the hands-on work.

### Can I caulk over existing caulk if it's not that bad?

I'd say no, and I mean that pretty firmly. Even caulk that looks okay often has micro-separations along the bond line that you can't see. New caulk over old caulk almost always fails faster than a fresh application on a clean surface. Pull it all out and start clean.

### Why does my caulk keep turning black and moldy so fast?

A few possible reasons: the surface wasn't fully dry when you applied it, you're using a non-mold-resistant caulk, or there's a ventilation problem in the bathroom that keeps humidity high. Look for caulk labeled "mold-resistant" (most silicone products are), and make sure your bathroom fan is actually moving air out of the house and not just into the attic.

### Is there a caulk I can actually paint over for a custom color match?

Yes. Paintable caulks exist, but they're latex-based and won't hold up in a submerged or constantly wet area. If you need a color match, look for siliconized acrylic caulk (like DAP Alex Flex) or the Polyblend grout caulk mentioned above. Pure silicone cannot be painted.

### When should I call a professional instead of doing this myself?

If you find soft drywall, black mold on the substrate behind the old caulk, or rotting wood when you strip the old bead, stop there and call someone. That's water damage that needs to be assessed and repaired before new caulk goes on. The caulk job itself is absolutely DIY-able -- what's behind the wall sometimes isn't.

---

A well-done caulk joint is one of those things that nobody notices. Which is exactly the point. Get it right and it'll quietly protect your walls and subfloor for years. Get it wrong and you'll be back here in six months, wondering why it's already peeling. Follow the prep steps even when they feel tedious, use real silicone, fill the tub first, and wait the full cure time. That's genuinely most of it.
