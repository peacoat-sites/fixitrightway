Tile failure from a bad subfloor costs the average homeowner $4,200 to rip out and redo, according to HomeAdvisor's 2025 contractor survey. Not from bad tile. Not from cheap grout. From skipping the prep.

I'll be honest: when I first started doing flooring installs in the early 2000s, I thought leveling was the kind of thing you could eyeball and fudge. I did that on a kitchen in a duplex renovation in 2004 and watched half those tiles crack within eight months. The grout joints opened up like little fault lines. That job taught me more than any trade school course.

The subfloor leveling step is where most DIY tile projects go wrong, and it's almost always because homeowners either don't know what "level" actually means in a tiling context, or they don't know what products to use. Let me fix that.

## What "Level" Actually Means Here

Here's where I see homeowners get confused. When tile installers talk about a "level" floor, they don't mean perfectly flat the way a carpenter's level shows you. They mean there are no high or low spots that exceed the tolerance the Tile Council of North America specifies. The TCNA Handbook sets that tolerance at 3/16 of an inch over a 10-foot span, or 1/8 inch over a 2-foot span for large-format tile (anything 15 inches or bigger on a side).

That number matters more than it sounds. A tile laid over a low spot has an unsupported center. When you step on it, it flexes. Tile doesn't flex. The result is a cracked tile or, more commonly, a cracked grout joint that slowly works its way into a trip hazard. What surprised me the most when I started measuring floors seriously is how many "flat-looking" subfloors actually fail that spec. I'd estimate 60 to 70 percent of the residential subfloors I've assessed in older homes (pre-1990 construction) need at least some correction.

Level and flat are also not the same as "doesn't slope." A bathroom floor can slope toward a drain and still be flat in the technical sense. You're not necessarily trying to make it perfectly horizontal everywhere. You're trying to eliminate the humps and dips.

## The Diagnosis Step Nobody Does Carefully Enough

Before you mix a single bag of anything, you need to actually map the floor. Get a 6-foot or 10-foot straightedge (a cheap aluminum one from Home Depot runs about $22) and drag it slowly across the floor in multiple directions. Mark every low spot with a pencil or chalk. This is tedious and nobody wants to do it, but it's the single most important thing you can do before deciding what products to buy.

While you're down there: check for bounce. Kneel or stand in different spots and shift your weight. Any springiness or bounce is a structural problem, not a leveling problem. Self-leveling compound cannot fix a joist that's undersized or a subfloor that's undersupported. Bouncy floors need a framing fix first. I've seen people pour $300 worth of self-leveler over a springy subfloor and then wonder why their tiles cracked in six months.

Also check for moisture. If you're working over a concrete slab (common in basements and slab-on-grade homes), get a calcium chloride moisture test kit. Florock and Ardex both make affordable ones, around $15 to $20. Most self-leveling compounds require moisture vapor emission rates below 5 lbs per 1,000 sq ft per 24 hours. Exceed that and you're looking at adhesion failure no matter how perfectly flat the floor is.

## Products, What They Cost, and When to Use Each

This is where it gets practical. Not every low spot calls for the same product.

As of August 2026, Ardex K-15 has become my go-to for most slab applications because of how it handles around doorways and transitions. Henry 547 is slightly easier to find at big-box stores, though.

One thing worth knowing about self-leveling compounds: they're sensitive to temperature and drafts. The product direction says 65 to 80°F ambient temperature. I've seen bags pumped in a cold garage in November seize up before they could flow. The water-to-powder ratio matters too. A little too much water and the compound is weak; too little and it won't flow into corners. Mix it with a corded drill and a paddle mixer, not a hand stir.

## The Actual Leveling Process, Step by Step

Clean the floor first. Completely. Dust, adhesive residue, and paint contamination will cause self-leveler to debond. I use a floor scraper for adhesive blobs, then vacuum, then wipe with a damp mop and let it dry. If you're working over concrete, apply a primer like Ardex P 51 or Henry 356 and let it get tacky. This step is not optional. Unprimed concrete will suck moisture out of the leveler before it can cure properly, causing cracking and weak spots. I skipped primer once on a large commercial bathroom remodel (long story involving a miscommunication with my assistant) and we had to grind out and redo a 12-foot section. Lesson absorbed.

Mix the compound in a 5-gallon bucket with your paddle mixer for about 2 minutes until it's smooth, then pour it into the low areas immediately. You have maybe 15 to 20 minutes of working time with most products. Use a gauge rake (a $25 to $40 tool with adjustable depth pins) to spread it to the right depth, then let it flatten itself. That's why they call it self-leveling.

For high spots: don't try to cover them with leveler. Grind them down first. A 7-inch angle grinder with a diamond cup wheel works for small humps. For anything bigger than about 4 square feet, rent a floor grinder. The dust is considerable, so wear a respirator rated N95 or better.

Let the compound cure fully before walking on it or laying backer board. Most products are firm enough to walk on in 2 to 4 hours but shouldn't have heavy traffic or tiling started for at least 24 hours. Check the manufacturer data sheet, not the bag front.

**A few worked examples from real projects:**

Master bath over a 1962 slab, 80 sq ft, three low spots between 1/4" and 3/4" deep. Applied two bags of Ardex K-15 with primer, floor rented at $0 (owned the gauge rake). Total material cost: $128. Zero tile failures in two years of follow-up.

Kitchen remodel over plywood subfloor, 220 sq ft, multiple dips from swollen OSB seams. Used Mapei Planipatch on the seams and transitions, then Ditra uncoupling membrane over the full floor instead of traditional backer board. Total leveling material: $94. The Ditra added $210 but eliminated any remaining flex concern. Client has had no issues.

Basement bathroom on a slab with a 1.25" low spot near the floor drain. Portland cement mortar bed set to level, allowed 72 hours to cure, then tiled directly. Material cost for the mortar: $34. Extra time investment: about 3 hours. No self-leveling compound would have handled that depth cost-effectively without multiple pours.

### FAQ

#### How flat does a subfloor need to be for tile?
The TCNA standard is 3/16 of an inch over a 10-foot span, or 1/8 inch over 2 feet for large-format tile. Measure with a long straightedge before buying any leveling material, because some floors are much worse than they look.

#### Can I use self-leveling compound on a wood subfloor?
Yes, but you need a product designed for wood subfloors, like Mapei Planipatch or Ardex Feather Finish. Standard concrete self-levelers will crack over wood because wood moves and they don't. Also check that your subfloor is at least 1-1/8" total thickness; thinner assemblies need structural reinforcement [before tiling](/how-to-level-an-uneven-floor-before-tiling/).

#### How long does self-leveling compound take to dry before tiling?
Most products allow foot traffic in 2 to 4 hours but need a full 24 hours before tiling. Some fast-setting products like Ardex K-15 are ready for tile in as little as 16 hours. Always check the technical data sheet, not the bag summary.

#### Do I need backer board if I've already leveled the floor?
Leveling and backer board solve different problems. Leveling fixes surface irregularities. Backer board (like HardieBacker or Schluter Ditra) addresses flex and moisture. Over wood subfloors, most tile installations still benefit from an uncoupling membrane or cement board even after leveling. Over concrete, a properly leveled slab can often accept tile directly.

#### What's the deepest low spot I can fill with self-leveling compound in one pour?
Most standard products are rated for 1 to 1.5 inches max depth. Going deeper in a single pour generates heat as it cures and can crack. For voids deeper than 1.5 inches, use a Portland cement sand mix to fill most of the void, let it cure, then skim the surface with self-leveler for flatness.
