Standing water at the bottom of your dishwasher after a full cycle is one of those problems that looks worse than it usually is. Most of the time, you don't need a technician. You need twenty minutes and a pair of rubber gloves.

The frustrating part is that most online guides send you straight to the drain hose or the pump motor, which are genuinely last-resort checks. Meanwhile the actual culprit, sitting right there under the lower rack, takes about four minutes to clean and fixes the problem probably 60% of the time. I've watched homeowners pay a $150 service call only to watch a tech pull out a chunk of wet food debris from the filter screen. That's the mistake we're going to avoid here.

Before you buy a part, before you call anyone, work through these checks in order. Each one takes less time than the next, and you'll stop the moment the problem's solved.

## Start with the Filter (Seriously, Start Here)

Sometime around 2010, manufacturers stopped putting self-cleaning grinders in most dishwashers and switched to manual-clean filters. Quieter machines, dirtier filters. If you haven't cleaned yours in the last few months, you may have found your problem.

Pull out the lower rack. Look at the bottom of the tub. You'll see a cylindrical mesh filter assembly, usually with a flat screen surrounding it. Most twist counterclockwise to unlock, Bosch, KitchenAid, Whirlpool, and GE all use some version of this. Pull the cylinder out, then lift the flat screen beneath it.

Run both pieces under hot water. Use an old toothbrush on the mesh. What you're looking for: packed grease, bits of food, or that distinctly gross gray film that builds up when the filter hasn't been touched in a year. I pulled one out of a client's Bosch 500 series last spring that was so compacted it looked like a lint screen from a dryer. Cycle had been leaving three inches of water for two weeks.

Reinstall the filter tight (you'll hear or feel it click into place if you've seated it correctly), run a short hot wash cycle, and check the result.

If the water drains: congratulations, clean your filter monthly. You're done.

If it doesn't: keep going.

## The Sump, the Drain Area, and What Else Gets Stuck There

With the filter out, look down into the sump opening, that's the cavity the filter sits in. Shine a flashlight in there. You're looking for broken glass, a small piece of plastic, a pit from a fruit, a twist tie. I've found all of these. Any solid object sitting on the drain impeller will block it completely.

If you can see something, use needle-nose pliers or your fingers to pull it out. Don't stick your hand in without looking first, broken glass is the reason.

While you're in there, manually spin the impeller (the small plastic fan visible through the sump opening on many machines). It should spin freely with light resistance. If it won't spin at all, it's either jammed or the pump motor has seized, which is a different problem we'll get to.

Also: check the air gap if your setup has one. It's the small chrome cylinder on your countertop next to the faucet. Unscrew the cap, pop the plastic cover, and look inside. A clogged air gap is one of those causes that almost never gets mentioned in guides, probably because not every kitchen has one, but when it's blocked it completely prevents draining. Clean it out with a brush or a pipe cleaner.

## Drain Hose: The Overlooked Middle Child

The drain hose runs from the pump, up and over (ideally in a high loop), and connects either to the air gap or directly to the [garbage disposal](/how-to-fix-a-garbage-disposal-that-wont-turn-on/) or sink drain. Two failure points here.

First, check whether the hose is kinked behind the machine. This requires pulling the dishwasher out a few inches, kill the power at the breaker first, disconnect it from the countertop screws, and slide it forward carefully. A kinked hose is obvious once you can see it. Straighten it, reroute it with a zip tie to the underside of the counter to hold the high loop, and you're done.

Second, and this trips people up, if you recently installed a new garbage disposal, there's a knockout plug inside the disposal's dishwasher inlet. It's there for units that don't have a dishwasher. If you didn't knock it out before connecting the drain hose, you've got a solid plastic wall where the water should go. This is so common that every disposal installation guide mentions it, and homeowners still miss it constantly. I missed it myself the first time I installed a disposal, and spent 40 minutes diagnosing the "wrong" problem before I caught it.

Knock it out with a screwdriver and hammer (from inside the inlet, with the disposal disconnected), fish the plug out of the disposal chamber before you run it, and reconnect.

If the hose itself is cracked, a replacement Whirlpool or universal dishwasher drain hose runs $12-$22 at any hardware store.

## How to Check and Replace the Drain Pump

If you've cleared the filter, the sump, the air gap, and the drain hose and you still have standing water, the drain pump is the likely culprit. This is where some people stop and call a tech, which is reasonable, but most drain pump replacements are genuinely DIY-able with basic tools and a YouTube video of your specific model.

The pump is at the bottom of the machine. You'll need to pull the dishwasher out fully, disconnect power (unplug or breaker), and often remove the lower spray arm and filter assembly to access it from inside the tub, or flip the machine on its back to access from below. The specifics depend on the brand. Bosch and Miele route their access differently than Whirlpool or GE.

| Brand | Pump Access | Avg. Part Cost (2026) | DIY Difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whirlpool / Maytag | Bottom panel or tilt machine | $35-$65 | Moderate |
| Bosch | Inside tub, below filter assembly | $70-$120 | Moderate-Hard |
| GE | Bottom panel removal | $40-$75 | Moderate |
| KitchenAid | Similar to Whirlpool | $45-$80 | Moderate |
| Miele | Usually requires Miele-authorized service | $95-$160 | Hard |
| Samsung | Bottom access | $50-$90 | Moderate |

Find your model number (usually on the door jamb inside the tub), search "[brand] [model number] drain pump replacement," and watch a video before you touch anything. The model number tells you which pump to order, don't guess. OEM parts from RepairClinic or Repair Clinic's competitors like PartSelect typically arrive in 2-4 days.

One thing nobody tells you: when you disconnect the pump, brace for residual water in the drain line to spill. Have a towel shoved in there before you pull the hose clamp. A shallow baking pan underneath helps too.

A drain pump replacement start to finish usually takes me about 45 minutes on a machine I've done before. Give yourself 90 minutes the first time, maybe two hours on a Bosch.

## When DIY Stops Making Sense

Three situations where I'd call a pro rather than keep digging.

The control board is throwing an error code that suggests an electrical drain failure, not a mechanical blockage. Boards on modern dishwashers run $80-$250 and diagnosing them without a multimeter and some wiring knowledge is guesswork.

Water is coming back into the tub after draining (called backflow), which can indicate a check valve issue or a plumbing problem outside the machine itself.

The machine is more than 12-14 years old and the repair cost approaches $200 or more. At that point you're maintaining an appliance that's living on borrowed time. A mid-range Bosch or Whirlpool runs $599-$799 today; sometimes the math just doesn't work.
