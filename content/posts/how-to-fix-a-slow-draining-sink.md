Your sink drains fine for six months, then one morning you're standing in two inches of soapy water while you brush your teeth. You know it's not a full clog yet. It's just... slow. And it's been slow for a while, and you've been ignoring it, and now here we are.

I've cleared hundreds of drains over the years, in everything from 1920s Craftsman bungalows with cast iron pipes to new construction with PVC. What most people don't realize is that a slow drain almost never requires a plumber. It's one of the most DIY-friendly problems in your whole house. The tools are cheap, the process takes maybe 20 minutes, and if you follow the right sequence you'll fix it without chemicals that quietly destroy your pipes.

Let me walk you through it the right way.

## Start With the Obvious: The Stopper and the Drain Opening

Nine times out of ten, a slow bathroom sink is a hair and soap scum situation. Not in the trap. Not in the wall. Right there at the drain opening, caught on the stopper or just below it.

Pull out your stopper first. Most bathroom sink stoppers twist counterclockwise and lift straight out. Some just lift with a bit of wiggle. What you'll find underneath is almost certainly a grey-black rope of compacted hair and soap that's been building since the last time anyone cleaned it. Pull it out with your fingers or needle-nose pliers and throw it away.

Once the stopper is out, shine a flashlight down into the drain. You'll often see a second clump sitting on the crossbar just below the opening. A bent wire hanger or a Zip-It tool (about $3 at any hardware store) will snag it. The Zip-It is a thin plastic barbed wand, totally disposable, and it is genuinely one of the most satisfying tools ever made. You'll pull out something disgusting and feel great about it.

Kitchen sinks are a different story. The culprit there is usually grease and food debris accumulating lower in the drain, which I'll cover below.

Clean and reinstall the stopper. Run the water. If the drain is still slow, keep going.

## The Plunger: Skipped Too Often

People reach for chemicals when their first move should be a plunger. I'd estimate that a good plunging session clears about 40% of the slow drains I see people overcomplicate.

You need a cup plunger, not a flange plunger. The flange plunger (with the rubber extension at the bottom) is for toilets. The flat cup plunger seats properly over a sink drain. If you only own one plunger, buy a second cup-style one and label it for sinks. Under $8 at Home Depot.

Here's the key step most people skip: block the overflow opening. That's the small hole near the top of your sink basin, usually oval or round. If you don't block it, you're just pushing air through the overflow instead of pressurizing the drain. Stuff a wet rag into it firmly and hold it there, or have someone else hold it.

Fill the sink with about two inches of water, seat the plunger over the drain, and do 10 to 15 firm pumps in quick succession. The water-to-water seal is what creates actual suction. Dry plunging doesn't work. Pull the plunger up hard on the last stroke. Run water immediately to test the drain rate. Repeat two or three times if needed.

## Cleaning the P-Trap (This Is Easier Than It Sounds)

If the stopper and plunger didn't fix it, you're probably dealing with buildup in the P-trap. The P-trap is the curved pipe section under the sink, shaped like the letter P on its side. It holds a small amount of water that blocks sewer gases from coming back up, and it's also where chunky debris collects.

Put a bucket under the trap before you do anything else. There's always water in there.

On PVC drains (white or grey plastic pipe), the trap usually has two hand-tightenable slip-joint nuts. Twist them counterclockwise to loosen, swing the trap down, and let the water drain into your bucket. On older metal traps, you may need channel-lock pliers and a gentle touch because the metal corrodes and the nuts can be brittle.

Look inside the trap. If there's a dark sludge or a solid buildup, that's your problem. Rinse it out with a garden hose outside or in a utility sink. While it's off, run a test by pouring water down the drain to confirm the blockage isn't further down the line. If water flows freely with the trap removed, the trap was the issue. If it still drains slowly with the trap off, the clog is past the trap in the branch drain or the stack, and now you're in snake territory.

Reinstall the trap hand-tight, give each nut a quarter-turn snug with pliers, and test. No plumber's tape needed on slip-joint connections, by the way. Those rely on the plastic or rubber washer inside the nut.

## Using a Drain Snake (When to Go Deeper)

A manual hand snake (also called a drum auger) costs $25 to $40 and handles most clogs that live in the branch drain, which is the horizontal run going into the wall. The Ridgid PowerSpin+ is around $30, takes a standard drill, and is my honest recommendation if you want one tool that covers most residential drain situations without going full plumber's-snake territory.

Feed the cable down the drain opening after removing the stopper, not through the P-trap cleanout (that's a less common setup). Turn the handle or drill clockwise as you push. When you feel resistance, you've hit the clog. Keep rotating and pushing gently. You'll either break it up or hook it and pull it back out. Don't force it aggressively if you feel hard resistance because old galvanized steel pipes and aggressive snaking can separate corroded joints.

For kitchen sinks specifically, the clog is usually a grease plug anywhere from 6 inches to 3 feet into the drain line. Hot water and dish soap flushed down the drain before and after snaking helps break up the grease and flush the loosened debris.

One opinion I'll stand behind even though I know some people disagree: skip Drano and similar chemical drain cleaners. I know they're everywhere and they're cheap and sometimes they seem to work. But sodium hydroxide drain cleaners generate heat when they react, they damage rubber gaskets and old pipes over time, and if they don't fully clear the clog you've now got a pipe full of caustic liquid that will burn you when you open the trap. The Zip-It plus a plunger plus a $30 snake handles 95% of what Drano gets sold for, with zero pipe damage and no hazmat situation.

## When to Actually Call a Plumber

If every single drain in your house is slow, or if clearing your bathroom sink makes your toilet gurgle, you're dealing with a main drain or venting issue. That's a pro call. Main line snaking runs around $150 to $300, and if the line has a bellied section or root intrusion you might be looking at a camera inspection (typically $100 to $200) before any work starts.

A single slow sink that you cannot clear after cleaning the stopper, plunging, removing the trap, and snaking 6 to 8 feet into the drain: that could mean a partial blockage deep in the line, a venting problem on that branch, or on older homes, a pipe that's partially collapsed or filled with scale. Call a plumber at that point. You've done everything a homeowner reasonably should.

Also: if you open the trap and find active corrosion, greenish staining on copper, or the pipe crumbles when you touch it, stop. Don't force anything. Get a plumber to assess it before you create a bigger problem.
