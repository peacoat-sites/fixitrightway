Your GFCI outlet keeps tripping, won’t reset, or simply stopped working altogether. It’s a frustrating problem, but the good news is that learning **how to replace a GFCI outlet** is one of the most manageable electrical tasks a homeowner can tackle. With the right tools, a little patience, and careful attention to safety, you can have a fully functioning outlet in about 30 minutes.

GFCI outlets,  short for Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter,  are those outlets with the small TEST and RESET buttons found in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, and outdoors. They protect you from dangerous electrical shocks, so keeping them in proper working order is essential for your home’s safety.

## What Is a GFCI Outlet and Why Does It Fail?

A GFCI outlet constantly monitors the flow of electricity through a circuit. The moment it detects an imbalance,  even a tiny one,  it cuts power in a fraction of a second to prevent electric shock.

Over time, GFCI outlets wear out from repeated tripping, power surges, moisture exposure, or simply old age. Signs that yours needs replacing include a RESET button that won’t stay in, no power at the outlet even after resetting, or a visibly cracked or discolored faceplate.

Replacing a faulty GFCI outlet is far safer than leaving it in place. A non-functioning GFCI offers zero protection against electrical shock.

## Tools and Materials You Will Need

Before you begin, gather everything you need so the job goes smoothly. Having the right tools on hand makes each step faster and safer.

  * New GFCI outlet (15-amp or 20-amp,  match your existing outlet)
  * Flathead screwdriver
  * Phillips head screwdriver
  * Non-contact voltage tester (essential for safety)
  * Needle-nose pliers
  * Wire stripper (optional, in case wires need re-stripping)
  * Electrical tape
  * Smartphone or camera to photograph existing wiring

You can find replacement GFCI outlets at any hardware or home improvement store for roughly $15–$25. Make sure you purchase the correct amperage,  check your circuit breaker panel if you’re unsure.

**Pro Tip:** Before buying your replacement outlet, take a photo of the old one still in the wall. This gives you a clear reference for wire connections and helps the hardware store associate confirm you’re buying the correct replacement unit.

## Step 1: Turn Off the Power and Test for Safety

This is the most important step in the entire process. Never skip it, and never rush through it.

Go to your home’s electrical panel and locate the breaker that controls the outlet you’re replacing. Flip it to the OFF position. If your breakers aren’t labeled, plug a lamp or phone charger into the outlet first, then flip breakers one at a time until the device loses power.

Once you believe the power is off, use your non-contact voltage tester at the outlet. Hold the tip near each slot of the outlet,  the tester will beep or light up if voltage is still present. Only proceed when the tester confirms absolutely no power is flowing.

**⚠️ Safety:** Never rely solely on flipping a breaker without confirming with a voltage tester. Mislabeled breakers are common in older homes, and working on a live circuit can cause serious injury or death. A non-contact voltage tester costs under $20 and is an essential safety investment.

## Step 2: Remove the Old GFCI Outlet

With the power confirmed off, use your flathead screwdriver to remove the outlet cover plate. Set the cover and its center screw aside in a safe place.

Next, use your Phillips head screwdriver to remove the two mounting screws holding the outlet to the electrical box,  one at the top and one at the bottom. Gently pull the outlet away from the wall, giving yourself several inches of slack in the wiring.

Before disconnecting anything, take a clear photo of all the wire connections with your smartphone. This is your roadmap for reconnecting the new outlet correctly. Label the wires with a small piece of tape if it helps you keep track.

## Step 3: Disconnect the Wires

GFCI outlets typically have two sets of terminals labeled LINE and LOAD. The LINE terminals connect to the wires coming from the breaker panel,  this is the power source. The LOAD terminals connect to wires feeding other outlets downstream, if applicable.

Loosen the terminal screws and carefully remove each wire. Most standard wiring uses three conductors: a black wire (hot), a white wire (neutral), and a bare copper or green wire (ground). Use needle-nose pliers to straighten any wire ends that have become bent or kinked.

If your outlet has wires connected to the LOAD terminals, make a note,  or mark them clearly,  because those wires need to connect to the LOAD terminals on your new outlet to maintain protection for downstream outlets.

## Step 4: Connect the New GFCI Outlet

New GFCI outlets come with the LOAD terminals covered by a piece of tape. If you are not connecting any downstream outlets, leave that tape in place,  it keeps those terminals safely capped.

Connect your wires to the new outlet in this order:

  * **Black wire (hot)** → LINE terminal marked HOT or brass-colored screw
  * **White wire (neutral)** → LINE terminal marked NEUTRAL or silver-colored screw
  * **Bare copper or green wire (ground)** → Green ground screw

Tighten each terminal screw firmly until the wire doesn’t pull free when you give it a gentle tug. Loose connections are a leading cause of outlet failures and can create fire hazards, so take your time here.

If you have wires for downstream outlets, connect those to the LOAD terminals in the same hot/neutral/ground pattern, and remove the protective tape from those terminals first.

## Step 5: Mount the Outlet and Restore Power

Carefully fold the wires back into the electrical box, tucking them in neatly so they aren’t pinched. Press the new GFCI outlet into the box and secure it with the two mounting screws,  top and bottom. Don’t overtighten, as this can crack the outlet’s plastic housing.

Attach the cover plate and tighten its center screw. Now head back to the breaker panel and flip the circuit breaker back to the ON position.

Return to the outlet and press the RESET button firmly until you hear or feel it click into place. Use your voltage tester or plug in a small lamp to confirm the outlet is working. Then press the TEST button,  the outlet should lose power immediately. Press RESET again to restore it. Your new GFCI outlet is fully installed and operational.

## When to Call a Licensed Electrician

While replacing a GFCI outlet is a straightforward DIY task for most homeowners, there are situations where calling a professional is the smarter choice.

Contact a licensed electrician if you encounter any of the following:

  * The wiring in your walls is aluminum (silver-colored) rather than copper
  * You see burn marks, melted insulation, or a burning smell inside the electrical box
  * You find more than three wires and can’t determine which are LINE versus LOAD
  * The new GFCI outlet trips immediately and repeatedly after installation
  * Your home’s wiring is knob-and-tube (common in homes built before 1940)

There is no shame in calling a professional when a situation exceeds your comfort level. Electrical safety is never worth compromising.
