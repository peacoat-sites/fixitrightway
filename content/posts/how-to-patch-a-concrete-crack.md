Most concrete cracks can be repaired successfully by a careful homeowner. The job takes a few hours, costs between $20 and $150 in materials depending on the method, and doesn't require a permit. What it does require is correctly diagnosing the crack first, because using the wrong repair product is the single most common reason patches fail within a year.

Concrete cracks fall into two broad categories: dormant and active. Dormant cracks have stopped moving; they're stable because whatever caused them (shrinkage during curing, a one-time load, thermal expansion) has resolved. Active cracks [are still](/ira-rebates-are-still-on-the-table-how-to-claim-them-now/) widening or shifting, usually because of ongoing soil movement, freeze-thaw cycling, or structural stress. Patching an active crack with rigid filler is a waste of time. The patch will pop out or crack alongside it, often within one season.

## Reading the Crack Before You Buy Anything

Width matters, but it's not the whole story. Here's a simple decision framework:

A hairline crack (under 1/16 inch) in a slab or wall that shows no displacement and hasn't grown is almost always a shrinkage crack from the original cure. These are cosmetic and common. A flexible polyurethane caulk or a thin epoxy injection kit handles them well.

A crack between 1/8 and 1/2 inch is where most homeowners are working. If the two sides are level (no vertical step between them) and the crack is dormant, a vinyl concrete patcher or hydraulic cement works fine for horizontal surfaces. For vertical walls, a two-part epoxy paste holds better than any cement-based product.

Vertical displacement, meaning one side of the crack sits higher than the other, is a red flag. So is a stair-step pattern in a brick or block [foundation wall](/how-to-seal-cracks-in-a-basement-foundation-wall/), which often signals differential settlement. Those conditions aren't a DIY patch situation. Get a structural engineer or a licensed foundation contractor to look before you spend a dime on materials.

One simple way to test for active movement: bridge the crack with a small piece of painter's tape and write today's date on it. Check it monthly. If the tape tears or pulls away from one side, the crack is still moving.

## Choosing the Right Repair Material

| Crack Type | Best Product | Approx. DIY Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hairline (< 1/16") surface | Polyurethane concrete caulk | $8–$15/tube | Paintable, flexible, stays watertight |
| Narrow (1/16"–1/8") | Epoxy injection kit | $30–$60/kit | Rigid bond; ideal for structural slabs |
| Medium (1/8"–1/2") horizontal | Vinyl concrete patcher | $15–$30/bag | Mix with water; feathers well at edges |
| Medium (1/8"–1/2") vertical | Two-part epoxy paste | $20–$45 | Doesn't sag; sands smooth when cured |
| Wide (1/2"+) or spalling | Hydraulic cement | $12–$25/bag | Expands as it sets; good for wet cracks |
| Active crack | Polyurethane backer rod + flexible sealant | $15–$30 | Rigid patch will fail; use flexible repair |

Epoxy injection kits (Polygem is one brand; similar kits are available at most big-box stores) include surface ports, the two-part resin, and instructions. They're genuinely good products for basement floor cracks or garage slabs where you want a structural bond. The limitation is that they're not forgiving of damp concrete. If moisture is seeping through the crack, hydraulic cement goes in first to stop active water, and then you can consider epoxy over the top once it's dry.

## The Repair Process, Step by Step

Good prep does most of the work. Count on spending at least as much time preparing the crack as actually filling it.

**1. Clean the crack.** Use a cold chisel and hammer or an angle grinder with a diamond blade to slightly widen and undercut the crack edges (a process called "keying" the crack). You want an inverted V shape so the patch has something to grip. Blow out all dust with compressed air or a shop vac. Skip this and patch adhesion drops significantly.

**2. Check for moisture.** Wipe the inside of the crack with a dry cloth. If moisture returns within a few minutes, you have water infiltration. Use hydraulic cement first. If you're using epoxy, the surface must be dry for a minimum of 24 hours.

**3. Apply a bonding agent if using cement-based filler.** Brush undiluted concrete bonding adhesive (Quikrete's Concrete Bonding Adhesive is widely available) into the crack and let it get tacky, about 10 to 20 minutes. Skip the bonding agent and your vinyl patcher may eventually delaminate.

**4. Pack the filler.** For cement-based products, mix to a stiff consistency, not soupy. Force it into the crack with a margin trowel, working in sections if the crack is long. Overfill slightly. For epoxy paste on a vertical crack, apply in layers no thicker than 1/4 inch to avoid slumping.

**5. Feather and finish.** For a horizontal slab you plan to leave as-is, smooth the patch flush with a trowel and lightly texture it to match the surrounding concrete. If the slab is painted, let the patch cure fully (at least 28 days for cement-based products) before painting.

**6. Cure time.** Don't rush this. Hydraulic cement sets fast, 3 to 5 minutes, but it's not at full strength for days. Vinyl concrete patcher needs at least 24 hours before foot traffic, 72 hours before vehicle traffic. Cold weather slows all of this down; below 50°F you'll want to tent and heat the repair area.

**Example:** A 6-foot crack in a basement floor, roughly 3/16 inch wide and dormant, with no moisture. Using a vinyl concrete patcher at 1 bag ($18), bonding adhesive at $9, and a tube of polyurethane caulk to seal the edges once cured ($12): total material cost around $39. Time: 2 to 3 hours including prep. No permit needed. This is a clear DIY job.

## When to Call a Pro

Permits aren't typically required for cosmetic concrete patching on a slab or driveway. But a few situations change that calculus entirely.

Cracks in a foundation wall that show signs of bowing or horizontal cracking (not diagonal, not stair-step, but horizontal across a block wall) indicate lateral soil pressure and may need a licensed structural engineer and permits for any repair. Carbon fiber straps, wall anchors, or piering systems all fall under professional territory and often require permits depending on your municipality.

Cracks in a pool shell require a licensed pool contractor in most states. Cracks in a public sidewalk or municipal sidewalk panel abutting your property: check with your city first, because repair responsibility and permit requirements vary by jurisdiction.
