You filed your injury claim on a Tuesday. By Friday, you'd already posted a photo from your cousin's backyard barbecue, smiling, holding a beer, standing on the leg you swore was too painful to walk on. Your insurance adjuster saw it by Monday morning. I've watched this exact sequence sink claims that were completely legitimate, and the frustrating part is that the injured person had no idea they were doing anything wrong. They weren't lying. They were just having a good day and sharing it the way everyone does now. But "the way everyone does now" happens to be one of the most effective weapons insurance companies have ever had access to.

## Why Insurance Adjusters Love Your Social Media More Than You Do

Let's be direct about what's happening on the other side of your claim. Insurance companies routinely assign investigators or use software tools to monitor claimants' public social media activity. This isn't occasional or targeted at suspicious claims. It's standard practice, especially on anything involving soft tissue injuries, back injuries, or pain and suffering damages where there's no clean X-ray to argue with.

What most people don't realize is that "private" doesn't mean protected. Adjusters know how to work around privacy settings through mutual connections, screenshots shared by third parties, or simply by checking what was public before you thought to lock things down. Courts have also repeatedly ruled that social media content is discoverable in litigation, meaning your attorney may eventually be ordered to hand over posts, messages, and photos even if they're no longer visible to the public.

The stakes are real. In one well-documented case type, a plaintiff claiming severe back limitations was photographed in a social media post helping a friend move furniture. The claim wasn't fraudulent. The person was having a better day than usual. But that single photo became the centerpiece of the defense's argument, and the jury agreed.

## The Seven Most Damaging Things People Post During Active Claims

I've seen all of these. Some of them multiple times.

**Photos of physical activity.** This is the obvious one, but it catches people constantly. A hiking photo, a dance floor shot at a wedding, carrying a child, even gardening pictures. Your claim says you can't perform normal daily activities. The photo says otherwise, and context doesn't survive a courtroom.

**Check-ins at gyms, sports venues, or recreational facilities.** You don't even have to post a photo. Just checking in at a bowling alley or a CrossFit gym during an active injury claim creates a problem your attorney will have to spend hours explaining.

**Venting about the accident or the other party.** "I can't believe that jerk ran the red light and now he's trying to say it was my fault" feels justified. It also hands opposing counsel a narrative about your emotional state, your characterization of disputed facts, and potentially admissible statements about the accident itself.

**Relationship and social updates that contradict emotional distress claims.** If your claim includes components for anxiety, depression, or loss of enjoyment of life, a string of cheerful posts about dinners out and weekend trips creates a direct conflict.

**Anything about the case itself.** Settlement negotiations, frustrations with your attorney, opinions about the insurance company, updates on depositions. I've watched clients torpedo their own settlements by venting online about the process.

**Tagged photos from other people.** You don't post it, your friend does, and suddenly you're in the picture. You need to monitor this and ask people close to you not to tag you in anything during an active claim.

**Old posts that resurface.** Discovery requests can go back years. If you had a pre-existing condition and your social media shows you were already limiting certain activities before the accident, that timeline becomes very useful to the defense.

## What to Actually Do the Moment You File a Claim

Here's a practical sequence. Do this before you post anything else, and ideally before you even tell many people about the accident.

**Step 1: Do not delete anything already posted.** This is the counterintuitive one. Deleting social media posts after a claim is filed can be characterized as spoliation of evidence, which is a legal concept that can result in sanctions against you or negative jury instructions. Talk to your attorney before you touch anything.

**Step 2: Screenshot and preserve everything yourself.** Your own posts, messages related to the accident, and anything the other party has posted. Date and save it all. This protects your interests too.

**Step 3: Tighten your privacy settings immediately.** Set all profiles to the most restrictive settings available. This isn't a guarantee, but it reduces casual exposure. Make sure past posts are also set to "friends only" or equivalent, not just future ones.

**Step 4: Tell your close circle.** Ask family and friends not to tag you, post photos of you, or mention your accident, recovery, or claim online. You can't control what they've already posted, but you can stop the bleeding going forward.

**Step 5: Consider a full pause.** I know this sounds extreme, but the cleanest solution is to simply stop posting for the duration of your active claim. No content means no risk. If that's not realistic for you, at minimum run every potential post through one question: could this be taken out of context to make me look less injured than I claim to be?

**Step 6: Discuss your existing accounts with your attorney at the first meeting.** Not as a confession, just as a practical disclosure. A good personal injury attorney will want to know what's out there so they're not surprised later.

## The "It Was Just One Post" Trap

The thing about social media evidence is that it doesn't need to represent your entire recovery. It just needs to create doubt. And creating doubt is a much lower bar than proving fraud.

Insurance adjusters and defense attorneys are very skilled at what I'd call "selective framing." They take your best moment, the one good afternoon you had, and they present it as representative. They ask questions in depositions designed to make you look like you're minimizing a photo rather than explaining a complex medical reality. Chronic pain doesn't work in straight lines. You can have an 8-out-of-10 pain day and a 3-out-of-10 day in the same week. That's completely normal and medically documented. But photos don't capture pain levels. They capture what you look like when you're smiling.

What's particularly unfair is that staying off social media entirely can also be used against you in some jurisdictions, with opposing counsel suggesting that the sudden absence of posts suggests consciousness of guilt. I've seen this argued. It rarely works, but it's one more reason to get an attorney involved early so your strategy is coherent rather than reactive.

## When Your Old Posts Come Back for You

Discovery in personal injury litigation can reach back two to five years on your social media history, sometimes further if a judge grants it. This matters most in two situations.

Pre-existing conditions are the big one. If your lower back was bothering you before the accident, your social media may already contain evidence of that: mentions of doctor visits, photos where you're visibly uncomfortable, posts about missing activities because of pain. The defense will use this to argue your current condition isn't entirely the accident's fault. This doesn't necessarily kill your claim, but it changes the math. Your attorney needs to know about it.

The second situation involves lifestyle claims. If you're claiming significant loss of quality of life and your pre-accident social media shows an active, high-energy lifestyle, that actually helps establish your baseline. But if your pre-accident posts show you were already sedentary, rarely social, and frequently mentioning health issues, the gap between before and after becomes much smaller in the defense's framing.

The takeaway is that social media isn't just a problem going forward. It's a record of who you were before the accident, and how that record gets used depends entirely on what's in it and who's doing the framing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I just make my accounts private after I file my claim?

You can and should tighten your privacy settings, but don't do it under the assumption it makes existing content invisible or legally protected. Courts can order production of private posts during discovery, and investigators sometimes capture public content before people think to lock their accounts. Setting things to private is a reasonable step, but it's not a solution by itself and it shouldn't be confused with deleting content, which carries its own legal risks.

### What if someone else tags me in a photo that could hurt my claim?

Ask them to untag you immediately, and if possible, ask them to remove the post. Be calm and explain why without getting into details about your case that could spread further. Going forward, change your settings so that you have to approve any tags before they appear on your profile. Most major platforms offer this. It doesn't remove a post from the tagger's profile, but it keeps it off yours and reduces the chance an investigator finds it through your account.

### My attorney told me to deactivate my accounts. Should I?

If your attorney advises this, they likely have a specific reason based on your case facts. Deactivating is different from deleting: deactivating suspends your account but preserves the data, while deleting destroys it. Deleting content after litigation has commenced or a claim has been filed can be treated as destruction of evidence. Always follow your attorney's specific guidance on this, and make sure you understand whether you're deactivating or permanently deleting.

### Does posting about my recovery progress hurt my claim?

It can, even when the intent is positive. "Doing better today, took a short walk" sounds innocent, but the defense will argue you're recovering faster than claimed. "Still in a lot of pain" could be cited as you diagnosing yourself or exaggerating. Anything about your physical status is risky. Medical updates belong in conversations with your doctor, not on Instagram.

### What if the other party or their insurance company is posting things too?

Screenshot everything and send it to your attorney immediately. The same logic works both ways. If the at-fault driver is posting about the accident, disputing facts publicly, or if their behavior on social media suggests they're not taking the situation seriously, that can be useful evidence. Your attorney can advise on whether and how to preserve it properly so it's actually admissible.

---

The honest truth is that most people who damage their injury claims on social media aren't being dishonest. They're just living their lives and sharing it the way a billion other people do every day. The problem is that injury claims create a window of time when normal behavior gets scrutinized under an adversarial microscope, and most people don't know that window exists until something goes wrong. The best move is a simple one: treat your social media like it's being read by the person who most wants to pay you nothing. Because it probably is.
