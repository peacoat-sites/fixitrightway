You wake up at 2 a.m. to the sound of dripping water. Not a flood, not a burst pipe, just that maddening, metronomic drip coming from your bathroom faucet. You jam a pillow over your head and tell yourself you'll deal with it tomorrow. Three weeks later, you're still "dealing with it tomorrow," and your water bill just jumped $40. That drip you're ignoring is wasting somewhere between 3,000 and 5,000 gallons of water a year, depending on the drip rate. I've seen homeowners convince themselves a leaky faucet is a plumber's problem when, nine times out of ten, it's a Saturday morning fix that costs under $20 in parts.

## What Kind of Faucet Do You Have? (This Changes Everything)

Before you buy a single part or pick up a wrench, you need to know what you're working with. This is the step most people skip, and it's why they end up at the hardware store three times in one afternoon.


## How Much Does Fixing a Leaky Faucet Cost?

| | DIY (parts only) | Professional |
|---|---|---|
| **Fix dripping faucet** | $5–$25 | $100–$250 |
| **Replace outdoor faucet** | $15–$40 | $100–$250 |

Most faucet repairs cost under $25 in parts. A plumber typically charges $100–$250 for the same job. See the [full home repair cost guide](/home-repair-cost-guide/) to compare costs across 30+ repairs.

There are four main faucet types you'll encounter in residential homes:

**Ball faucets** are single-handle faucets where the handle rotates around a ball-shaped cap. These are extremely common in kitchens. When they leak, it's usually because the springs or seats inside have worn out.

**Cartridge faucets** can be single or double handle. The cartridge is a self-contained unit that controls water flow. When it goes bad, you replace the whole cartridge, which is actually great news because it makes the repair simple.

**Ceramic disc faucets** use a wide cylindrical body with a single lever. They're durable and relatively rare to find leaking, but when they do, you're usually cleaning or replacing the ceramic disc.

**Compression faucets** are the oldest style, with two separate handles you tighten down to stop water flow. These are the ones leaking in older homes and rental properties everywhere. They wear out the most often, and they're also the easiest to fix.

To figure out which you have: look at the handle configuration and movement. Two separate handles that you tighten to turn off? Compression. Single handle that moves up and down or side to side? Probably cartridge or ball. Single handle on a wide, cylindrical base? Likely ceramic disc.

## Where Is the Faucet Actually Leaking From?

The location of the leak tells you almost everything. I've had clients spend an hour fixing the wrong part because they didn't stop to look carefully.

**Drip from the spout when the faucet is off:** The internal valve or seal is worn. This is the classic scenario, the 2 a.m. drip.

**Leak at the base of the spout:** The O-rings around the spout body are shot. This usually shows up as water pooling around the base when the water is running.

**Leak around the handle:** The packing nut or stem O-ring is worn. You'll see water seeping out from under or around the handle.

**Leak under the sink at the supply line connections:** This is less about the faucet itself and more about the connection fittings. Sometimes it's as simple as hand-tightening a nut a little more.

Knowing exactly where the water is coming from will tell you which parts to buy before you ever disassemble anything.

## How to Fix the Most Common Types (Step by Step)

I'll walk you through compression and cartridge repairs, since those cover the vast majority of what homeowners encounter.

### Fixing a Compression Faucet (The Classic Drip)

**What you'll need:** Adjustable wrench, Phillips and flathead screwdrivers, replacement washers and O-rings (get an assorted pack for $6-8), plumber's grease, and possibly a seat wrench if the valve seat is damaged.

1. **Shut off the water supply.** There are shut-off valves under the sink, one for hot and one for cold. Turn them clockwise until they stop. Then open the faucet to release pressure and drain any remaining water.
2. **Remove the handle.** Pry off the decorative cap on top of the handle with a flathead screwdriver. Underneath you'll find a screw, usually Phillips head. Remove it and pull the handle straight up.
3. **Remove the packing nut.** Use your adjustable wrench to unscrew the large hexagonal nut underneath the handle. Turn counterclockwise.
4. **Pull out the stem.** The stem is the threaded rod that comes up out of the valve body. Turn it counterclockwise to unscrew it, or in some cases it will just pull out.
5. **Inspect the washer.** At the bottom of the stem, you'll see a rubber washer held in by a brass screw. If it's flat, cracked, or deformed, that's your culprit. Unscrew it and take it to the hardware store to match the exact size. They're typically $0.50-1.00 each.
6. **Check the O-ring on the stem.** While you're in there, look at the O-ring around the middle of the stem. Replace it if it looks worn. Coat the new one in plumber's grease before installing.
7. **Inspect the valve seat.** Reach into the valve body with your finger and feel for roughness or pitting. A damaged seat will chew through new washers quickly. If it feels rough, you'll need a seat wrench to remove and replace it, or you can use a seat dresser tool to smooth it out. This is a $15 tool that saves you from repeat failures.
8. **Reassemble in reverse order.** Put the new washer in, thread the stem back in, tighten the packing nut, reattach the handle.
9. **Turn the water back on slowly.** Check for leaks at the supply valves, at the base of the faucet, and at the handle. Run the water for a minute, then shut it off and watch the spout.

### Fixing a Cartridge Faucet

This one is even more straightforward. The tricky part is getting the right replacement cartridge for your specific brand. Write down the faucet brand before you go to the store, or pull the cartridge out first and bring it with you.

1. Shut off supply valves and relieve pressure.
2. Remove the handle by prying off the decorative cap and removing the handle screw.
3. Look for a retaining clip or nut holding the cartridge in place. Remove it.
4. Pull the cartridge straight up. Some are stiff. You can buy a cartridge puller for about $10, or use needle-nose pliers to grip it and wiggle gently.
5. Take note of the cartridge orientation before pulling, or take a photo.
6. Insert the new cartridge in the same orientation.
7. Reassemble, restore water, and test.

## When to Call a Plumber Instead of DIYing It

I'm going to be straight with you: most faucet repairs are genuinely DIY-able. But there are specific situations where you should put down the wrench and make a call.

**Call a plumber when:**

- The shut-off valves under the sink won't close fully. If they're corroded open, you'll need to shut off the main water supply and may have a bigger valve replacement job on your hands. A plumber can run $150-300 for this, but it needs to be done right.
- You discover the leak is coming from the supply lines themselves and you see corrosion, green buildup, or the braided line has any visible damage. Flexible supply lines should be replaced every 10 years anyway, but a corroded connection close to the wall is not a homeowner repair.
- You open up the faucet and find the valve seat is cracked or the faucet body itself is damaged. At that point, you're often better off replacing the entire faucet, which is still a DIY-possible job for many people but jumps in complexity.
- You're not comfortable working with the plumbing at all. A plumber charging $100-200 for an hour of work is still cheaper than the water damage from a botched repair.

The honest truth is that if you can follow instructions and you're not intimidated by turning a wrench, 80% of leaky faucet repairs are within reach. What most people don't realize is that the actual repair work is less than 20 minutes. It's the prep, the parts run, and the reassembly that take time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it actually take to fix a leaky faucet?

For a compression washer replacement, the hands-on repair work takes 15-25 minutes once you have the right parts. Budget an extra 30-60 minutes for your hardware store trip if you don't have parts on hand. Cartridge replacement is similar. The first time you do it always takes longer because you're figuring it out. The second time, it'll feel routine.

### Do I need to turn off the main water supply to fix a faucet?

Not usually. Most sinks have individual shut-off valves directly below the faucet, connected to the hot and cold supply lines. Turn those clockwise to close them. If those valves are stuck, corroded, or missing entirely, then yes, you'll need the main shut-off. The main shut-off is typically located near the water meter, in a basement, or in a utility closet.

### My faucet still drips after I replaced the washer. What did I miss?

The most likely culprit is a damaged valve seat, which is the part inside the faucet body that the washer presses against when you turn it off. If the seat is pitted or rough, it will shred new washers quickly. You'll need a seat wrench to remove and replace it, or a seat dresser to resurface it. Another possibility: you got the wrong size washer. Even slightly off is enough to cause problems. Bring the old one to match it exactly.

### How do I know if I need to replace the whole faucet vs. just repair it?

A few indicators lean toward full replacement: the faucet is very old (20-plus years) and parts are hard to source, the body of the faucet is cracked or corroded, or you've repaired the same faucet twice in a year and it keeps failing. Also, if your current faucet is a builder-grade fixture that cost $30 originally, a $40 repair doesn't make sense when a better replacement faucet costs $60-80 and comes with a warranty.

### Can a leaky faucet cause water damage under my sink?

Yes, and it happens more often than people expect. A faucet that's leaking at the base or at a supply connection can drip slowly into the cabinet below, warping the cabinet floor, promoting mold growth, and eventually damaging the subfloor. I've seen what looked like a minor faucet leak turn into a $2,000 cabinet and subfloor repair because nobody checked under the sink. Any time you notice a leak, open the cabinet doors and look at the condition of the wood down there.
