You were crossing the street, doing everything right, and then your world changed in an instant. Maybe you're reading this from a hospital bed, or maybe you're home now but still in pain, staring at a pile of medical bills and a notice from an insurance adjuster who wants to talk. You might be wondering whether you even have a real claim, whether it's worth the hassle, or whether the driver's insurance company is actually on your side. Here's the honest truth: pedestrian accident claims are among the most serious and complex personal injury cases out there, and the decisions you make in the first few days can either protect you or cost you significantly.

## Why Pedestrian Claims Are Different From Other Car Accident Cases

When two cars collide, both drivers typically have insurance, both have similar bargaining positions, and the physical damage to each vehicle tells part of the story. When a car hits a person, the equation is completely lopsided. The vehicle sustains minor damage. The human sustains broken bones, traumatic brain injuries, internal bleeding, and in many cases, permanent disability. The financial stakes are much higher, which means the insurance company's motivation to minimize your claim is also much higher.

Pedestrian claims involve multiple layers of potential liability. The driver who hit you is the obvious starting point. But you might also have a claim against a municipality if a broken crosswalk signal or missing signage contributed to the accident. A property owner whose overgrown hedge blocked the driver's sightline could share liability. In some cases, vehicle defects play a role. I've seen cases where peeling away these layers made the difference between a modest settlement and one that actually covered the injured person's future.

There's also the question of comparative fault. Depending on your state, an insurance company may argue that you were partly responsible because you weren't in a marked crosswalk, you were wearing dark clothing, or you stepped off the curb without looking. Some states use "pure comparative negligence," which means you can recover even if you were 99% at fault, with your award reduced by your percentage of fault. Others use "modified comparative negligence," which cuts off your right to recover if you're found more than 50% or 51% at fault. Knowing your state's rule matters enormously before you say anything to an insurance adjuster.

## What to Do in the First 72 Hours

The actions you take immediately after a pedestrian accident shape everything that comes later. I know that's a lot of pressure when you're injured and frightened. But these steps are concrete, and each one protects you.

**Step 1: Get medical care immediately, even if you feel okay.**
Adrenaline masks pain. Traumatic brain injuries, internal bleeding, and soft tissue damage often don't feel serious in the first hours. Go to the emergency room or urgent care. Let doctors document everything. This creates the medical record that connects your injuries to the accident, which is the backbone of your claim.

**Step 2: Call the police and get a report number.**
Even if the driver wants to "handle it privately," insist on a police report. That report records the driver's information, any citations issued, witness statements, and the officer's observations about conditions at the scene.

**Step 3: Document the scene if you're physically able, or ask someone to help you.**
Photographs of the intersection, crosswalk markings, traffic signals, skid marks, the vehicle, and your injuries are critical. Take them before anything gets cleaned up or repaired.

**Step 4: Get witness contact information.**
People scatter quickly. Ask bystanders for their names and phone numbers before the scene disperses.

**Step 5: Notify your own insurance company.**
Your own auto insurance policy may include uninsured motorist coverage or MedPay (medical payments coverage) that could apply, even though you weren't in a car. This surprises a lot of people.

**Step 6: Don't give a recorded statement to the driver's insurance company yet.**
This is the step most people get wrong. The adjuster who calls you within 24 to 48 hours is friendly, sympathetic, and looking for language they can use to reduce your claim. You are not legally required to give that statement. Politely decline until you've spoken with an attorney.

## Understanding What Your Claim Is Actually Worth

This is usually the first real question people have, and it's fair. You need to know what you're dealing with financially.

Pedestrian injury claims typically include two categories of damages: economic and non-economic.

**Economic damages** are the quantifiable losses. These include all medical bills (emergency care, surgery, hospitalization, physical therapy, follow-up visits), lost wages from time you couldn't work, future lost earning capacity if your injuries affect your career long-term, and costs for home modifications, medical equipment, or ongoing care needs.

**Non-economic damages** are harder to put a number on but often represent the larger portion of the settlement in serious cases. These include pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment of life, and in some states, loss of consortium for your spouse or partner.

Here's what I tell people who ask me to just give them a ballpark: a broken arm with clean recovery might settle for $30,000 to $80,000. A traumatic brain injury or permanent disability can result in settlements or verdicts in the millions. The severity of injury, the clarity of liability, the insurance policy limits available, and the skill of your representation all factor in. Policy limits are often the ceiling. If the driver who hit you carries only the state minimum liability coverage, which in many states is just $25,000, that cap may be your practical reality unless you pursue other avenues like underinsured motorist coverage or additional defendants.

## The Insurance Company's Playbook (And How to Counter It)

Insurance adjusters are not bad people, but they work for the insurer, not for you. Their job is to resolve your claim for as little money as possible. Understanding their common tactics helps you stay ahead of them.

**The quick settlement offer.** Many adjusters will make an offer early, before you've finished treatment or understood the full extent of your injuries. Accepting it closes your case permanently. If complications emerge six months later, you have no recourse. Never settle before you've reached maximum medical improvement, meaning your doctors have a clear picture of your long-term prognosis.

**The recorded statement trap.** As I mentioned, anything you say can be used to suggest you were partially at fault or that your injuries aren't that serious. "I'm doing okay" in casual conversation can appear in a claim file as evidence you downplayed your symptoms.

**Delay tactics.** Some insurers drag their feet, hoping financial pressure will push you into accepting less. Knowing your state's statute of limitations (typically two to three years for personal injury, but it varies) means you understand the actual deadline, not the pressure deadline.

**Disputing causation.** Insurers may argue your injuries were pre-existing or unrelated to the accident. Your complete and continuous medical documentation is your best defense against this argument.

## When to Handle It Yourself vs. When to Hire an Attorney

| Situation | DIY or Attorney |
|---|---|
| Minor injuries, fully recovered, clear liability | Possible to DIY with careful documentation |
| Lost more than a few days of work | Attorney strongly recommended |
| Any broken bones, surgery, or hospitalization | Attorney strongly recommended |
| Traumatic brain injury or permanent impairment | Attorney is essential |
| Disputed liability or comparative fault arguments | Attorney is essential |
| Municipality or employer involved as potential defendant | Attorney is essential |
| Death of a family member (wrongful death) | Attorney is essential |

Most personal injury attorneys take pedestrian cases on contingency, meaning you pay nothing upfront and they collect a percentage (typically 33% to 40%) of your settlement or award only if they win. For serious injuries, the math almost always works in your favor. Studies and industry data consistently show that represented claimants receive significantly higher settlements, even after attorney fees, than unrepresented claimants.

That said, if your injuries were genuinely minor, you recovered quickly, and the driver's liability is clear, you may be able to negotiate directly with the insurance company. Just make sure you've reached maximum medical improvement and have all your bills documented before making any offer or accepting any settlement.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do I have to file a pedestrian accident claim?

The statute of limitations for personal injury claims varies by state, but most fall between two and three years from the date of the accident. Some states allow less time (Louisiana allows one year), and cases against government entities often require a formal notice of claim within 90 to 180 days of the accident. Missing these deadlines typically means losing your right to any compensation entirely, so treat them as hard stops, not soft guidelines.

### What if the driver who hit me was uninsured?

This is more common than most people realize, with roughly 13% of drivers nationwide uninsured. If the driver has no insurance or insufficient insurance, your own auto insurance policy may provide uninsured or underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage, even if you weren't in a vehicle at the time. Renters and homeowners policies sometimes provide additional avenues. An attorney can help identify all available coverage and defendants.

### Can I still make a claim if I was partly at fault for the accident?

In most states, yes. Pure comparative negligence states allow recovery regardless of your percentage of fault, reduced by that percentage. Modified comparative negligence states (the majority) allow recovery as long as you were less than 50% or 51% at fault. Only a handful of states still use contributory negligence, which bars recovery if you were even 1% at fault. The adjuster's argument that you share blame is common, but it's a negotiating position, not a final ruling.

### Should I accept the first settlement offer from the insurance company?

Almost never, especially if you haven't finished treatment. First offers are routinely low because insurers know many people don't understand what their claim is actually worth or don't want the hassle of negotiating. The offer also typically requires you to sign a release that closes your case permanently. Once you're at maximum medical improvement and have a complete picture of your losses, you (or your attorney) are in a much better position to evaluate whether a number is fair.

### What if I was hit in a parking lot or private property, not a public street?

Your claim still exists. Liability still flows from the driver's negligence regardless of whether the accident occurred on a public road or private property. The property owner may also share liability if poor lighting, confusing traffic flow design, or inadequate signage contributed to the accident. Police may not respond to accidents on private property in some jurisdictions, so documenting the scene yourself becomes even more important.
