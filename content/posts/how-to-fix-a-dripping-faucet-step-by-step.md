Your faucet won’t stop dripping, and that steady _drip, drip, drip_ is driving you absolutely crazy. More importantly, that tiny drip is quietly wasting hundreds of gallons of water each year — and adding dollars to your water bill every month. Learning **how to fix a dripping faucet** is one of the most rewarding home repairs you can tackle, and with the right guidance, most seniors can complete this job in under two hours without calling a plumber.

This step-by-step guide walks you through the entire process clearly and safely. Whether you have a ball faucet, a cartridge faucet, or a classic compression faucet, the core principles are the same. Let’s get that drip fixed once and for all.

## Why You Should Fix a Dripping Faucet Right Away

A faucet that drips just once per second wastes more than 3,000 gallons of water per year, according to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. That’s not just bad for the environment — it’s money straight down the drain.

Beyond the water waste, a persistent drip can cause mineral staining in your sink, lead to corrosion around the drain, and even signal a larger plumbing problem developing underneath. Fixing it early saves you from a much bigger repair bill later on.

## Tools and Materials You Will Need

Before you start, gather everything you need so you won’t have to stop midway through. Having all your supplies ready makes the job go smoothly and keeps you from struggling with frustrating interruptions.

Here is what to have on hand:

  * Adjustable wrench or basin wrench
  * Phillips-head and flathead screwdrivers
  * Needle-nose pliers
  * Replacement washers and O-rings (assorted kit works well)
  * Replacement cartridge or ball assembly (if needed)
  * Plumber’s grease (silicone-based)
  * White vinegar (for mineral deposit cleaning)
  * A small bucket or towel
  * Flashlight or headlamp
  * Non-slip mat to kneel or stand on

Most of these items are available at any hardware store for a very reasonable price. An assorted washer and O-ring kit typically costs less than ten dollars and covers most common faucet repairs.

**Pro Tip:** Before you go shopping, take a photo of your faucet’s brand name and model number (usually found under the sink or on the faucet body). Showing that photo to a hardware store employee will help you find exactly the right replacement parts the first time.

## Step 1: Identify What Type of Faucet You Have

Not all faucets work the same way, so identifying your faucet type is the very first step. The three most common types in homes are compression faucets, ball faucets, and cartridge faucets.

**Compression faucets** have two separate handles — one for hot and one for cold. They use rubber washers that wear down over time, making them the most common cause of dripping in older homes.

**Ball faucets** have a single handle that rotates over a rounded ball-shaped cap. They are very common in kitchens and contain several small parts including springs, seats, and O-rings.

**Cartridge faucets** can have one or two handles and use a cartridge mechanism inside. They are extremely common in modern homes and are often the easiest to repair — you simply replace the cartridge.

## Step 2: Shut Off the Water Supply

This is the most important step, and skipping it will result in a very wet and stressful experience. Look under your sink for the shut-off valves — there should be one for hot water and one for cold water. Turn them both clockwise until they stop.

If you cannot find individual shut-off valves under the sink, you will need to shut off the main water supply to your home. The main shut-off is usually located in the basement, garage, or near your water meter.

**⚠️ Safety:** Always turn on the faucet after shutting off the supply valves to release any remaining water pressure in the pipes. This prevents a sudden rush of water when you open up the faucet and protects you from an unexpected splash. Place a towel or small bucket in the sink to catch residual drips.

Once the water is off and pressure is released, you are ready to disassemble the faucet safely.

## Step 3: Disassemble the Faucet Handle

Most faucet handles are held in place by a decorative cap and a single screw. Use a flathead screwdriver to gently pop off the decorative cap on top of the handle — it usually just pries off with a little gentle pressure.

Underneath the cap, you will find a screw. Remove it with the appropriate screwdriver, then gently pull the handle straight up and off. If it feels stuck, wiggle it slightly side to side — never force it, as this can crack older handles.

Once the handle is removed, you will see the internal mechanism. Depending on your faucet type, this will be a stem, cartridge, or ball assembly. Take a moment to look at everything carefully before removing any more parts.

This is also a great time to take a photo with your phone so you remember how everything goes back together. A simple photo saves a great deal of confusion during reassembly.

## Step 4: Replace the Worn Parts

This is where you actually fix the problem. The exact part you replace depends on your faucet type, but the process is straightforward for all three.

**For compression faucets:** Unscrew the packing nut with your wrench and pull out the stem. At the bottom of the stem you will find a rubber washer held by a brass screw. Remove the old washer, take it to the hardware store to match the size exactly, and install the new one. While you are there, replace the O-ring on the stem as well.

**For cartridge faucets:** Pull the cartridge straight out — you may need needle-nose pliers to grip it. Take the old cartridge to the hardware store and buy an identical replacement. Insert the new cartridge in the same orientation as the old one.

**For ball faucets:** This type has the most parts. You will find springs, rubber seats, and O-rings inside the ball housing. Replace all of them using a ball faucet repair kit — these kits include everything and are sold specifically for your faucet brand.

Before installing new rubber parts, apply a thin coat of silicone-based plumber’s grease to each O-ring and washer. This helps them seat properly and extends their lifespan significantly.

## Step 5: Reassemble and Test the Faucet

Now it’s time to put everything back together. Work in reverse order from how you took it apart — this is where that photo you took earlier becomes very helpful. Tighten all screws and nuts snugly, but do not overtighten them.

Once everything is reassembled, slowly turn your shut-off valves back on by rotating them counterclockwise. Turn the water on gradually to avoid a sudden pressure surge through the new parts.

Turn on the faucet and check carefully for any drips or leaks. Run both hot and cold water separately and watch all around the base of the handle and under the sink. If you see a small drip, try tightening the packing nut just slightly more.

In most cases, the drip will be completely gone. Give yourself a well-deserved pat on the back — you just saved money on a plumber and became a little more self-sufficient at home.

## When to Call a Plumber Instead

While most dripping faucets are fixable with the steps above, there are times when it makes good sense to call in a professional. If you notice any of the following, it is wise to stop and get expert help.

  * The pipe fittings under the sink look corroded or show signs of rust
  * Water is dripping from beneath the sink rather than just the faucet
  * The shut-off valves under the sink will not turn or are leaking themselves
  * You have low water pressure throughout the house
  * The faucet body is cracked or visibly damaged
  * You feel uncomfortable or unsteady bending under the sink

There is absolutely no shame in calling a plumber. Your safety and comfort always come first. But for a standard dripping faucet, the repair described here is genuinely within reach for most homeowners.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does it take to fix a dripping faucet?

Most dripping faucet repairs take between 30 minutes and two hours for a first-time DIYer. If you have all your parts ready before you start, you can often complete the job in about 45 minutes. Cartridge faucets tend to be the fastest to repair, while ball faucets take the most time due to the number of small parts involved.

### How much money can I save by fixing a dripping faucet myself?

A plumber typically charges between $100 and $250 to fix a dripping faucet, depending on your location. The parts you need usually cost between $5 and $25. By doing it yourself, you could easily save $100 to $200 on a single repair — and the skills you learn will help you handle future plumbing issues with confidence.

### My faucet is still dripping after I replaced the washer. What should I do?

If the drip continues after replacing the washer, the seat — the metal surface the washer presses against — may be worn or pitted. You can purchase a seat wrench to replace the seat yourself, or have a plumber resurface it with a special tool. In some cases, particularly with very old faucets, it may be more cost-effective to replace the entire faucet rather than continuing to repair individual components.
