Water pooling at the base of your toilet means one of three things: the [wax ring](/how-to-replace-a-toilet-wax-ring-step-by-step/) has failed, the toilet is loose on the floor, or there's condensation dripping down and collecting. The first two need a fix today. The third is mostly a nuisance. Knowing which problem you have changes everything about the repair.

Here's the quick diagnosis. Press firmly on both sides of the toilet bowl near the floor. If the toilet rocks even slightly, that movement is the likely culprit. A toilet that shifts with use grinds away at the wax ring over time, eventually breaking the seal. A stable toilet with water consistently appearing after a flush points more directly to a failed wax seal. And if the water appears even when the toilet hasn't been used recently, check whether the tank is cold to the touch, because condensation is the simpler explanation.

## First, Rule Out the Easy Stuff

Before you shut off the water supply and start pulling the toilet, spend two minutes confirming it's actually a wax ring or flange problem and not something simpler.

Dry the floor completely. Flush once, then watch where the water appears. If it comes from under the base of the toilet, you're looking at a wax ring or flange issue. If it trickles down from somewhere higher, like the connection between the tank and bowl, or from the supply line fitting at the wall, that's a different repair entirely and doesn't involve the floor at all. Don't pull a toilet off a good wax ring to fix a leaking fill valve.

Also check that the two bolts at the base of the toilet (called closet bolts or johnny bolts) are visible and accessible. Sometimes the plastic caps are snapped over them. If those bolts spin freely or the nuts underneath them are corroded away, that's often why the toilet is loose in the first place.

## What You're Actually Dealing With: The Wax Ring and Flange

The wax ring is a soft wax gasket that sits between the bottom of the toilet and the floor flange (the round fitting that connects the toilet to the drain pipe below). When you set a toilet, the wax ring compresses and creates a watertight seal. That seal can fail because the toilet moved, because the flange is cracked or sitting too low, or simply because the ring dried out over many years.

The flange matters more than people think. A flange that sits more than about a quarter inch below the finished floor surface won't allow the wax ring to compress properly, and you'll get a slow leak no matter how many wax rings you go through. If your tile was installed after the toilet was originally set, there's a decent chance the flange is now sitting low. In that case, a standard wax ring won't cut it and you'll need either a thicker wax ring, a stacked double ring, or a wax-free foam gasket (brands like Fernco and Fluidmaster make these).

A cracked or broken flange is a different problem. If you pull the toilet and find the flange is broken or the mounting ears are snapped off, you'll need to repair or replace it before reinstalling. That can mean anything from a simple repair flange ring ($10–20 at any hardware store) to cutting into the floor to replace the flange entirely. That second scenario is where I'd say most homeowners should bring in a plumber.

## The Replacement Process, Step by Step

This is where most of the detail lives, because the actual work is what trips people up.

**What you'll need:** An adjustable wrench, a putty knife, rubber gloves, old towels or rags, a bucket, a new wax ring, and new closet bolt hardware (replace them while you're in there, they're cheap).

**1. Shut off the water supply valve** at the wall behind the toilet. Flush to empty the tank and bowl. Use a sponge to remove the remaining water in the bowl and tank. This keeps things cleaner and lighter.

**2. Disconnect the supply line** from the bottom of the tank. Have a towel ready.

**3. Remove the caps and nuts** from the closet bolts at the base. If the nuts won't budge, apply penetrating oil and wait ten minutes. If the bolts spin in the flange slot, grip them with pliers while you turn the nut.

**4. Lift the toilet straight up** and set it on its side on old cardboard or towels nearby. A standard toilet weighs 60–120 pounds. A one-piece toilet can weigh 90–130 pounds. If you've got a two-piece, it's faster and easier to remove the tank first (two bolts on the underside of the tank connect it to the bowl).

**5. Scrape off the old wax** from both the toilet horn (the opening at the bottom of the bowl) and the flange. Get it all. This is where the rubber gloves earn their keep.

**6. Inspect the flange.** Look for cracks, broken ears, and check whether it sits at or slightly above the finished floor. If it's broken, deal with that now before proceeding.

**7. Drop new closet bolts** into the flange slots. Position them so they line up with the holes in the toilet base.

**8. Press the new wax ring** onto the toilet horn (not onto the flange). Warm wax compresses better. If your house is cold, let the wax ring sit at room temperature for 30 minutes first.

**9. Lower the toilet straight down** onto the flange, with the bolt holes aligned. Don't wiggle it once it touches. Set your weight on it gently to compress the wax.

**10. Hand-tighten the washers and nuts** on the closet bolts, alternating sides, then snug them with a wrench. Do not overtighten. The toilet base is porcelain and it will crack. Snug is enough.

**11. Reconnect the supply line, turn the water on**, and flush several times. Watch the base. If it's dry after three flushes, you're done. Apply caulk around the base if you want (optional, but it prevents surface water from getting under there), leaving a small gap at the back so if a leak ever does develop, water will show on the floor instead of rotting it invisibly.

## When You Should Not Do This Yourself

The wax ring swap itself is accessible to a careful, patient homeowner. But stop and call a professional if:

The floor around the toilet base feels soft, spongy, or gives when you step on it. That's subfloor damage, possibly into the floor joists, and it needs a contractor to open the floor and assess the damage before the toilet goes back.

You pull the toilet and discover the flange is below the floor surface, broken, or the pipe below it is PVC glued to a cast iron flange. Cutting out and replacing a flange in a cast iron stack requires specific tools and experience with both plumbing and working in tight spaces.

You're not comfortable with the possibility that you'll need to make an unplanned trip to the hardware store mid-job, or that the first attempt might not seat perfectly and you'll need to repeat it. The job requires patience more than skill, but it does require both.
