My gas bill last January was $340. The month before I insulated my garage door, that same bill had been $410. Same house, same thermostat settings, same Minnesota winter. That $70 difference sounds modest until you add it up across a heating season and realize I spent about $85 on materials.

If your garage shares a wall with your living space, or if you've got a bedroom above it, or if you just use the garage for anything other than parking a car and hoping for the best, insulating that door is probably the highest-return weekend project you're not doing.

Here's what most people don't realize: a standard uninsulated steel garage door has an R-value somewhere between R-2 and R-4. A basic foam board insulation kit gets you to R-8. A good batt kit or a two-inch polystyrene install can push you to R-12 or R-16. That's not a rounding error. That's a fundamentally different thermal envelope.

## What You're Actually Choosing Between

There are three realistic paths for DIY garage door insulation, and they are not equal.

**Foam board (rigid polystyrene or polyisocyanurate):** This is what I'd recommend for most people. You buy 4x8 sheets, cut them to fit the door panels, and secure them with adhesive or clips. Foil-faced polyiso from a brand like Owens Corning or Rmax gives you the best R-value per inch. Two inches of polyiso runs about R-13. A 4x8 sheet of 1.5-inch Rmax Thermasheath-3 costs around $28-$35 at Home Depot, and you'll need maybe 4-6 sheets for a standard 16x7 door. Total materials: $130-$200.

**Fiberglass batt kits:** Companies like Owens Corning and Clopay sell pre-cut batt kits specifically for garage doors, usually around $50-$70 for a single-car door and $90-$130 for a two-car door. The Owens Corning Garage Door Insulation Kit (two-car, R-8) sits around $95 at Lowe's right now. They're fast to install and they look neat. The downside: you're capped at whatever R-value the kit offers, the faced batts can absorb moisture over time if you live somewhere humid, and they add more weight than foam board, which matters for your door opener and springs.

**Reflective foil (radiant barrier):** I'll be blunt here. For most climates, reflective foil alone is not worth your time. It works on radiant heat transfer, which matters in a hot attic or a Florida garage in August, but in a cold climate where you're trying to hold heat in, you need mass insulation, not a radiant barrier. If someone sold you a garage door insulation kit that's basically a layer of bubblewrap sandwiched in foil, you didn't get R-10. You got maybe R-2 on a good day. The research on foil bubble products is not kind.

My honest recommendation: foam board for cold climates and mixed climates, batt kits if you want a simpler install and you're in a moderate climate. Skip the foil-only products.

## What You'll Need Before You Start

Measure your door panels, not just the overall door. Most raised-panel doors have a frame rail around each panel, and the recessed area you're filling is smaller than the panel itself. I've watched people cut a stack of foam boards to the wrong dimension because they measured the outside of the panel instead of the recess. Measure each panel separately because they're not always identical, especially on older doors.

For a foam board install you'll need:

- Rigid foam (polyiso or EPS, at least 1.5 inches thick)
- A utility knife with fresh blades (foam board dulls them fast)
- A long straightedge or T-square
- Construction adhesive or foam board adhesive (Loctite PL300 works well, about $7 a tube)
- Garage door insulation clips if you prefer mechanical fastening (sold in packs of 10 for around $8)
- A tape measure
- Work gloves (foil-faced polyiso has sharp edges)

One more thing: weigh your door before and after, or at least be aware of the weight you're adding. Most openers handle the extra load from foam board just fine. Batt kits are heavier. If your opener is already straining, a full batt kit can tip it over the edge and you'll be replacing a motor or springs sooner than you planned. Springs are the bigger concern, actually. If your door was balanced for an uninsulated panel weight and you add 10-15 pounds of batts, you can throw off the spring tension. This doesn't always cause immediate problems, but it shortens spring life. If your springs are already several years old, this is worth thinking about.

## The Actual Install, Step by Step

This is the part where most guides get vague. Here's how I do it.

Start with the door closed and locked so it doesn't move on you. Work from the bottom panel up.

**Step 1: Measure each panel recess.** Write down height and width separately. For a typical two-car steel door with raised panels, the recesses are usually around 12-13 inches tall and 19-22 inches wide, but don't trust that. Measure yours.

**Step 2: Score and snap the foam board.** Lay the sheet on a flat surface. Score deeply with your utility knife along a straightedge, then snap it over the edge of your workbench. For polyiso specifically, score the foil facing on both sides before snapping or it'll tear ragged. You want a clean edge. If your cut is slightly off, a quick pass with a coarse sanding block smooths it out.

**Step 3: Test fit before you glue.** Slide the cut piece into the panel recess. It should fit with a little tension, not so tight you're forcing it. You want the edges to contact the frame rails. If it's loose, the adhesive still holds it, but tight is better.

**Step 4: Apply adhesive or clips.** If you're using adhesive, run a bead around the perimeter of the foam piece, about an inch in from the edge, plus an X across the center. Press it into the recess and hold it for 30-60 seconds. Foam board clips (they look like a small washer with a pin) get driven through the foam into the door panel. I prefer clips on the top panels because they're easier than reaching overhead with adhesive tubes.

**Step 5: Repeat for every panel, working up.** Let the door set for a few hours before running it open and closed.

**Step 6: Check clearances.** Open the door fully. Watch where the panels bend and stack. On most sectional doors the bend happens at the horizontal joint between panels, and the foam goes on the flat panel face, not the joint, so there's no conflict. But if you've over-filled or used foam that's too thick for your panel depth, you can get binding. This is why test-fitting before gluing matters.

The whole job on a two-car door takes most people 2-3 hours. I've done it in under 90 minutes on a door I'd done before.

## The Weight Problem Nobody Talks About

I keep coming back to weight because I've seen this bite people. A 16x7 door running two inches of polyiso on every panel adds roughly 8-12 pounds depending on foam density. That's generally fine. Two-inch fiberglass batts across the same door can add 25-30 pounds. Suddenly you're asking your torsion spring (or extension springs on older doors) to do significantly more work on every open cycle.

The fix, if you go with heavier insulation, is to have your springs adjusted after the install. This is a $75-$150 job at most garage door shops and it's worth it. Do not try to adjust torsion springs yourself. I mean it. These are under extreme tension and they can cause serious injury if you release them wrong. Extension springs on the sides of the door are less dangerous but still require caution. Let a pro handle the tension adjustment.

## Finishing Touches That Actually Matter

Insulating the panels is the big move, but the door frame is leaking too. Check the weatherstripping around all four sides. The bottom seal in particular takes a beating. A new vinyl bottom seal for a 16-foot door runs about $20-$30 and takes 20 minutes to replace. The side seals (called stop molding) should compress against the door face when it's closed. If there's a gap you can see daylight through, replace them. A full weatherstrip kit from a brand like Frost King runs $15-$25.

The top of the door frame, where it meets the header, often gets missed. If you're losing heat there, a piece of adhesive foam weatherstrip tape fixes it for a couple of dollars.

Also: none of this insulation does much if you have a door with a broken seal at the bottom or gaps around the frame that let outside air push straight through. Seal the envelope first, then insulate.
