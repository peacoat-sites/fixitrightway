Copper still wins in exactly one scenario: you're doing a small repair on an existing copper system and you don't want to deal with transition fittings. That's it. For anything bigger, for a partial repipe or a full house repipe, PEX has made copper look like a stubborn habit more than a smart choice.

I say that after spending years running both. The arguments you'll still find online defending copper for DIY work are mostly recycled from before PEX-A tools dropped in price and before anyone had two decades of field data on PEX reliability. That data exists now, and the picture is pretty clear.

## What You're Actually Choosing Between

PEX is cross-linked polyethylene tubing. It comes in three types: PEX-A (made with the Engel method), PEX-B (silane method), and PEX-C (electron beam). Most DIYers should use PEX-A. It's the most flexible, it has the best freeze resistance, and it's the only type that works with expansion fittings, which are stronger than the pipe itself at the joint. Uponor (formerly Wirsbo) and Rehau make the best PEX-A on the market. PEX-B from brands like SharkBite or Watts is fine for repairs and short runs, but I wouldn't build a whole house on it when PEX-A exists.

Copper is type M, L, or K. For residential work, you're almost always using Type M (thinnest walls, cheapest) or Type L (thicker, better for hot water lines under higher pressure). Type K is overkill for anything inside a house wall. Most plumbing supply houses and big-box stores stock Type M and L in half-inch and three-quarter-inch, which covers 90% of residential repipe work.

The honest comparison: copper is a known quantity. It's been in walls since the 1950s, code officials understand it, and a bad copper joint is usually visible because it drips or shows a green stain. PEX failures are rarer but when they happen, they're often slower and harder to catch.

## Cost, And Why The Gap Is Bigger Than You Think

Half-inch Type M copper runs around $1.10 to $1.40 per linear foot currently. Half-inch PEX-A runs $0.45 to $0.65 per foot. On a 1,500 square foot house, a full repipe might require 400 to 600 feet of half-inch pipe plus another 100 or so feet of three-quarter for mains. Do the math: you're looking at a $300 to $500 difference on pipe alone for an average house.

Fittings push that gap wider. A half-inch copper sweat elbow costs around $1.50 to $2.50. A PEX-A expansion elbow from Uponor costs about $1.00 to $1.50. But here's where it really diverges: a PEX manifold system, where you run individual supply lines from a central manifold to each fixture, uses almost no fittings at all in the walls. One connection at the manifold, one at the fixture. That's the home-run method, and it's the way I'd do a full repipe every time.

The tool cost is the catch. A PEX-A expansion tool from Uponor (the ProPEX expansion tool) runs $300 to $400 new. You can rent it at most tool rental places for around $40 to $60 a day. Copper tools, a decent pipe cutter and a propane setup with flux and solder, run about $80 to $120 and you own them forever. If you're doing one repipe and never touching plumbing again, rent the PEX-A tool. If you're a frequent DIYer, buy it. It pays back fast.

## The DIY Skill Gap Is Real

Sweating copper pipe properly takes practice. Not a ton of it, but enough that your first six or eight joints are going to make you nervous. The most common mistakes are not cleaning the pipe and fitting thoroughly, not applying enough flux, applying heat unevenly, or letting the pipe move while the solder is cooling. Any one of those gives you a joint that looks fine, passes a pressure test, and then weeps behind your drywall three years later.

I made exactly this mistake early in my career on a bathroom addition. The joint looked soldered. It wasn't. Found out when the homeowner called about a soft spot in their ceiling below. That repair cost more than the original job.

PEX-A with expansion fittings is genuinely forgiving. You heat the ring and pipe end with the expansion tool, slide it onto the fitting, and the pipe memory pulls it tight as it contracts back. The joint either seats correctly or it obviously doesn't. There's almost no middle ground where it looks fine but isn't. SharkBite push-to-connect fittings (which work on both copper and PEX-B) are even simpler, but I'd keep those to accessible locations only. They're not something I'd bury in a wall on a permanent run.

For a homeowner who has never done any plumbing, PEX-A is the right call. The learning curve is maybe two or three practice fittings before you feel confident. Copper with sweat fittings is a skill that takes a full afternoon of practice to get right, and getting it wrong has consequences.

## Code, Permits, and What Your Inspector Will Actually Say

Both materials are approved under the International Residential Code and have been for years. Most jurisdictions in the US allow PEX for supply lines without any asterisks. The exceptions: some older California counties and a handful of municipalities in the Northeast have historically been slower to warm to PEX, though most have updated their codes. Pull your local code or call your building department before you buy anything.

Permits for repipes are not optional. I know that's not what anyone wants to hear, but an unpermitted repipe is a liability when you sell the house, and a failed inspection catches things that matter, like improper support spacing (PEX needs more support than copper, every 32 inches vs. every 6 feet for copper horizontally), or mixing PEX and copper without the right dielectric considerations. Actually, PEX and copper play fine together, you just need a brass or copper adapter, not galvanized steel.

One thing inspectors sometimes flag on PEX: UV exposure. PEX degrades when exposed to sunlight over time. Any exterior run or any section near a window needs to be sleeved or covered. This is a real limitation that copper doesn't share, and it's worth knowing before you plan your routing.

## How I'd Actually Approach a Full House Repipe

Planning matters more than the pipe material. Start by mapping your existing supply lines, locate your main shutoff, your hot water heater, and every fixture. Count your fixtures: toilets, sinks, showers, tubs, dishwasher, washing machine, outdoor hose bibs, refrigerator line if applicable.

For PEX with the home-run manifold method, you'll place a manifold, a branch manifold from Uponor, Watts, or Sioux Chief, near your water heater. Each port feeds one fixture with an uninterrupted run of PEX. Half-inch for everything except the main incoming line and the hot water heater connections, which get three-quarter. Label every port on the manifold as you go. This sounds like overkill until you're standing there three years later needing to shut off just the kitchen sink.

Buy 10 to 15% more pipe than your measurements suggest. PEX routes differently than rigid pipe, it bends around framing, goes through holes, runs longer paths, and you'll make cuts that don't work out. Waste is built into the job.

For copper, the approach is trunk-and-branch: a main three-quarter trunk from the water heater, with half-inch branches tapping off at each fixture location. More fittings, more joints, more potential failure points. It's how most homes built before 2000 are plumbed, and it works, but the manifold method is cleaner from a maintenance standpoint.

Pressure test before you close up any walls. For PEX, cap all your open ends, pressurize the system to 80 to 100 PSI with an air compressor, and let it sit for two hours. Any drop tells you there's a problem. Find it before the drywall goes up.
