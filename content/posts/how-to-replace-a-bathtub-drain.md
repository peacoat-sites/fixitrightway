Your bathtub's draining slower every week. Maybe water pools around your feet in the shower now, or you've noticed a faint smell coming from under the tub. A corroded drain doesn't just annoy you, it quietly damages the wood and drywall beneath, and by the time you notice soft spots or staining, you're looking at thousands in water damage repair. The good news? You can replace it yourself. Even if you've never done this before, the process takes maybe an hour and costs $10 to $40 for the parts. This guide walks you through it step by step.

## Why Bathtub Drains Need Replacing

Every shower, your drain faces an assault: soap scum, hair, hard water minerals, constant moisture. The metal flange (that ring you see at the bottom of the tub) corrodes and loses its seal. Water seeps underneath and rots whatever's below.

Ignoring a failing drain is expensive. Fixing it now costs you time and a small kit from the hardware store. Waiting until the subfloor gives way costs you $2,000+ in foundation repair.

Your drain probably needs replacing if you've got rust or cracks visible on the flange, water that won't drain even after you've yanked out the hair, seeping water around the drain's base, a stopper that won't hold water, or an increasingly foul smell coming from down there.

## Tools and Materials You Will Need

Grab everything before you start. It's tedious to hunt for a flathead screwdriver halfway through.

Here's what you need:

  * **New bathtub drain kit** (available at any hardware store, check your drain size first, typically 1.5 inches)
  * **Drain removal tool or needle-nose pliers**
  * **Flathead screwdriver**
  * **Plumber's putty or silicone caulk**
  * **Adjustable wrench or channel-lock pliers**
  * **Flashlight or headlamp**
  * **Old towels or rags**
  * **Bucket**
  * **Plumber's tape (Teflon tape)**
  * **Rubber gloves**

Most drain kits run $10 to $40 at Home Depot or Lowe's. Take a photo of your old drain with you and show it to someone working there, they'll point you to the exact replacement. (I learned this the hard way by grabbing the wrong one the first time.)

**Pro Tip:** Before you go to the store, measure your drain opening and figure out whether your stopper is a toe-touch, lift-and-turn, or push-pull type. Matching styles means zero extra hassle and no adapters to fiddle with.

## Step 1: Prepare Your Work Area

Photo by [Zac Gudakov](https://unsplash.com/@zacgudakov?utm_source=seniorstrength&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=seniorstrength&utm_medium=referral)

Turn off the water supply to the tub. Find the shut-off valves behind the access panel if you can, or use the main house shut-off if not. You're working on the drain, not the pipes, but it's good practice.

Throw old towels around the tub's base to protect your floor and give yourself a cushioned place to kneel. Set a bucket nearby, trapped water sometimes spills out during removal.

If your tub has an overflow plate (that oval cover near the top), check whether your stopper mechanism connects to it. Some designs do, and you'll need to remove the plate first to release the linkage.

**⚠️ Safety:** Don't kneel in a dry, slippery tub. Lay down a non-slip bath mat or grip pad first. If you have balance issues or mobility concerns, skip the tub entirely and work from a sturdy stool beside it, or ask a family member to help. Falling is not worth proving you can do this alone.

## Step 2: Remove the Old Drain Stopper

| Stopper Type | Removal Method |
| --- | --- |
| Toe-touch | Open it. Unscrew the center post counterclockwise. Lift it out. |
| Lift-and-turn | Lift and twist counterclockwise until it releases. Some have a tiny set screw on the side that needs loosening with a flathead first. |
| Push-pull | Pull straight up and unscrew it counterclockwise by hand. |
| Trip-lever drain | Unscrew the overflow faceplate and pull the whole mechanism out through the opening. |

You can't access the drain itself until the stopper comes out. Method depends on what type you've got:

  * **Toe-touch stopper:** Open it. Unscrew the center post counterclockwise. Lift it out.
  * **Lift-and-turn stopper:** Lift and twist counterclockwise until it releases. Some have a tiny set screw on the side that needs loosening with a flathead first.
  * **Push-pull stopper:** Pull straight up and unscrew it counterclockwise by hand.
  * **Trip-lever drain:** Unscrew the overflow faceplate and pull the whole mechanism out through the opening.

Set the stopper aside. You might reuse it if it's still good, or your new kit might include a replacement.

## Step 3: Remove the Old Drain Flange

Photo by [Zac Gudakov](https://unsplash.com/@zacgudakov?utm_source=seniorstrength&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=seniorstrength&utm_medium=referral)

The flange is the threaded metal piece screwed into the pipe below your tub. This is what you're actually replacing.

Insert your drain removal tool (called a drain key or drain wrench) into the opening. Position its prongs against the crossbars inside, then turn counterclockwise to unscrew the flange. No drain tool? Needle-nose pliers in the crossbars will work, though a proper tool gives you way more grip.

The old drain's probably stuck from years of putty and rust. Use steady, gentle pressure instead of forcing it. If it won't budge, spray some penetrating oil around the rim and wait 10 minutes.

Once it's out, scrape away all the old putty and gunk with a putty knife or your gloved fingers. Bare, clean edges mean your new drain seals properly.

## Step 4: Install the New Drain Flange

Roll a rope of plumber's putty about pencil-thick and press it around the underside lip of the new flange. If your kit says to use silicone instead (common with acrylic and fiberglass tubs), do that, putty can stain those materials.

Thread the new flange in by hand, turning clockwise. Once hand-tight, use your drain tool to tighten it more. Snug is right. Over-tight and you'll crack the tub surround. You'll see putty squeeze out around the rim, which is exactly what should happen.

Wipe away excess putty with a damp rag. If you used silicone, let it cure for 24 hours before running water through it.

## Step 5: Reinstall the Stopper and Test Your Work

Photo by [Masiyah](https://unsplash.com/@masiyah?utm_source=seniorstrength&utm_medium=referral) on [Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/?utm_source=seniorstrength&utm_medium=referral)

Reverse your removal steps to put the stopper back in. Tighten any set screws and make sure it moves smoothly between open and closed.

Turn the water on and watch around the drain. No seeping water around the flange? Check underneath the tub too if you can access it through a closet or crawlspace. A good drain shows zero leaks.

Fill the tub a few inches and wait five minutes without letting water drain. If the level stays put, you've done it.

## When to Call a Plumber Instead

This is genuinely doable for most people, but not always. If you see water damage or soft spots in the subfloor around the tub, that's a sign of a long-standing leak that needs professional eyes on it.

Same goes for a cracked or corroded drain pipe below floor level. That's beyond DIY territory. Asking for help isn't weakness, it's knowing when to bring in someone with the right tools.
