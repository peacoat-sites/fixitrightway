A [running toilet](/how-to-fix-a-running-toilet-that-wont-stop/) costs the average household somewhere between $70 and $200 extra per year on their water bill, and that's for a *slow* runner. A fast one, the kind that hisses constantly and never fully stops, can waste 200 gallons a day. I've walked into houses where the owners had no idea why their water bill jumped $40 a month, and within about 90 seconds I could hear the toilet down the hall. You learn to tune in to that sound.

I'll be honest: this is one of those repairs that gets massively overcomplicated online. Most running toilet tutorials act like you're going to need a plumber and a permit. In 20 years, I've fixed probably 300 of these things, and the vast majority took under 30 minutes and a $12 part from a hardware store. But there's a smaller subset that will absolutely fool you, and those are the ones that send homeowners to YouTube at 11pm getting increasingly frustrated.

What surprised me was how many running toilets get misdiagnosed. People replace the flapper first (usually the right call), but when that doesn't fix it, they give up or call a plumber. The real answer is almost always in the fill valve, and that's a $10 to $18 swap that anyone who can turn a nut can do.

## Why Your Toilet Is Running (And How to Tell Which Problem You Have)

There are three main causes of a running toilet: a worn flapper, a bad fill valve, or a float set too high. They feel similar from the outside but have different tells. Here's how to read the symptoms.

**Constant running after every flush:** This is almost always the flapper. The flapper is the rubber disc at the bottom of the tank that seals water in until you flush. When it wears out or warps, it doesn't seat properly, and water trickles continuously into the bowl. You'll often hear a faint hissing or see a slight shimmer in the bowl water. The dye test confirms it: drop five drops of food coloring into the tank (not the bowl), wait 10 minutes without flushing, and check the bowl. Any color in the bowl means the flapper is leaking.

**Phantom flushing (runs briefly, randomly, without being touched):** Same culprit, the flapper, but a slower leak. The tank drains down slowly until the fill valve triggers a short refill cycle. Because it only runs for 15 or 20 seconds every hour or so, people often don't realize anything is wrong until they see the water bill.

**Constant running that never stops even if you don't flush:** This is usually either the float set too high (so water spills into the overflow tube) or a failing fill valve. Take the tank lid off and look at the overflow tube, the tall plastic tube in the middle of the tank. If water is running into the top of that tube, your float is set too high. If water isn't going into the overflow tube but the toilet still runs, the fill valve is the problem.

I made this mistake myself early on, at a rental property I was managing on Roanoke Street around 2014. I replaced the flapper twice, different brands, both times, and the toilet kept running. Took me longer than I'd like to admit to pop the lid off and notice the fill valve was visibly cracked and spitting water sideways instead of filling cleanly. Ten dollars and fifteen minutes later, problem solved. The lesson: always take the lid off before you buy anything.

## Fixing a Bad Flapper

This is where you start 80% of the time. Shut off the water supply valve (the oval handle on the wall behind the toilet), flush to drain the tank, and unhook the old flapper from the ears on the overflow tube. They either lift off or unclip, depending on the style. Note the style before you head to the hardware store.

The Fluidmaster 502P7 is the part I've installed more than any other. It's around $6-$7 at Home Depot or Lowe's as of this year (July 2026 pricing), it fits almost every two-piece toilet, and it comes with detailed diagrams for the three or four ways the ears might attach. Korky makes a good universal model too, the Korky 100BP, which has a slightly thicker rubber that holds up well in older tanks where the seat has minor mineral deposits. I've had better luck with Korky in hard-water areas.

Snap the new flapper onto the overflow tube ears, hook the chain to the flush lever (leave about a half-inch of slack, not more, or it'll get caught under the flapper when you flush), turn the water back on, and test. If the bowl isn't getting any food coloring after 10 minutes, you're done.

One thing that catches people: the flapper seat itself, the rim the flapper sits against, can develop mineral buildup or small cracks. Run your finger around it. If it's rough or pitted, a new flapper won't seal correctly no matter how good it is. You can try scrubbing the seat with fine steel wool, but if it's visibly cracked, you'll need to replace the entire flush valve, which is a bigger job and the point where I'd consider calling a plumber depending on the toilet's age.

## Replacing the Fill Valve

If the flapper checks out but the toilet still runs, or if your diagnosis points to the fill valve, this fix is only slightly more involved. Budget 20-30 minutes.

The standard replacement is a Fluidmaster 400A, which runs around $11-$14, or the Fluidmaster 400AH (the "PerforMAX" version at about $15-$18) if you want the quieter, higher-capacity version. Both are sold at every hardware store and most big-box home improvement retailers.

Shut off the supply, flush to drain, then use a towel to soak up what's left in the tank. There will be residual water, maybe half an inch, and you will definitely drip it on yourself when you pull the fill valve. That's just how it goes. Use a pair of channel-lock pliers (Channellock 426 or similar) to unscrew the locknut under the tank, outside the bowl. The whole valve lifts out.

The new fill valve has an adjustable height. Set it per the instructions to sit about an inch below the top of the overflow tube, then drop it into the hole, tighten the locknut hand-tight plus a quarter turn (don't overtighten, these tanks crack), hook the refill tube to the clip on the overflow tube, and turn the water back on. Adjust the float arm or float adjustment screw until the tank fills to about an inch below the top of the overflow tube.

Here's the insider detail: the refill tube that comes with the Fluidmaster 400A is often too long. If it drops down into the overflow tube instead of clipping to the outside of it, it creates a siphon that drains your tank slowly. Clip it to the outside lip of the overflow tube, or trim it if needed. This one issue probably accounts for 20% of the "I replaced the fill valve and it still runs" complaints I hear.

## Adjusting a Float Set Too High

If water is visibly running into the overflow tube, this is the fastest fix. No parts needed.

On older toilets with a ballcock assembly (the kind with a ball float on a metal or plastic arm), you can often bend the arm slightly downward to lower the water level. On modern fill valves, there's usually a screw adjustment on the valve body, or a pinch clip and twist mechanism on the float. Adjust until the water sits about an inch below the top of the overflow tube.

→ A reader in Phoenix last year described a toilet that had been running for "months, maybe longer." Turned out the float arm had corroded and was waterlogged, so the ball float no longer floated. It sat low, tricked the fill valve into staying open, and water poured constantly into the overflow. Replaced the fill valve and float assembly together: $16 in parts, 25 minutes of work. Her next water bill dropped by $31.

## When to Stop DIYing and Call Someone

Most running toilet fixes are genuinely within reach for a homeowner who can use a wrench. But there are situations where I'd pick up the phone.

If you find a crack in the porcelain tank, anywhere, that's a replacement situation, not a repair. If the flush valve seat (the plastic ring the flapper sits on) is cracked or pitted badly enough that no flapper will seal, and the toilet is a one-piece model or a pressure-assist type (common in condos and newer construction), the internal parts are expensive and the tolerances are tighter. A plumber can often do that job faster and cleaner than a homeowner who hasn't done it before.

Also: if you've replaced both the flapper and the fill valve and the toilet still runs, something else is going on. Could be a cracked overflow tube, a hairline crack in the tank, or a supply line issue. At that point, a plumber's diagnostic eye is worth the $75 or so most charge for a service call.

→ Scenario: A homeowner replaces the flapper, toilet still runs. Replaces the fill valve, toilet still runs. Calls a plumber. Plumber finds the overflow tube has a crack at the base where it meets the flush valve, and water was bleeding through. Parts plus labor: $187. The DIY attempts weren't wasted (those parts needed replacing eventually anyway), but the diagnosis needed a second set of eyes.
