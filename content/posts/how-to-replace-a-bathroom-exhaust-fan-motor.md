Most bathroom exhaust fan motors don't die all at once. They slow down first, get louder, start smelling faintly of something burning if you hold your hand near the grille. I've replaced probably 60 or 70 of these over the years, and I'll be honest: the first time I did one, I pulled the whole fan housing out of the ceiling because I assumed you had to. Completely wrong. Half the time you can swap just the motor and blower wheel assembly without touching the housing, without cutting any drywall, without even turning off a [circuit breaker](/how-to-reset-a-tripped-circuit-breaker/) for more than 20 minutes. That's the version most articles skip straight past.

What surprised me, though, was how many motors I've seen that didn't need replacing at all. Worn-out bearings that just needed a drop of 3-in-1 oil on the shaft. Blower wheels clogged so badly with lint that the motor was working against itself for years. Worth ruling those out first before you spend $18 to $65 on a new motor assembly.

## What You're Actually Replacing

The housing is the metal box in the ceiling. That stays. The motor is a small cylindrical unit that clips or screws into the housing, with a squirrel-cage blower wheel pressed onto the shaft. This motor-plus-blower assembly is what you're after. Brands like Broan-NuTone, Panasonic, and Delta have been making these for decades, and most of the major ones sell replacement motor kits designed to drop into their existing housings.

The model number is the thing. It's stamped or printed inside the housing on a label, usually on the side wall near the wiring connections. You'll see something like "Broan 688" or "NuTone 763N." Write that down, or photograph it. A lot of these motors cross-reference, so even a discontinued model will have a current equivalent, but you need the number to find it. I don't have great data on which aftermarket motors hold up long-term, so I tend to stick to OEM replacements when the cost difference is small.

## The Actual Replacement Process

Kill the power at the breaker. I can't tell you how many times I've watched homeowners flip the wall switch and call it good. Fan circuits frequently share a breaker with bathroom lights or outlet circuits, and the switch itself doesn't isolate everything in the box. Use a non-contact voltage tester (the Klein Tools NCVT-3 runs about $22 and is what I carry) before you touch any wires.

Pull the grille off the ceiling. It either snaps off spring clips or unscrews from a center screw hidden under a cap. Once it's down, look at how the motor assembly is mounted. On most Broan/NuTone fans, you'll see a metal bracket holding the motor against one side of the housing. There are usually two or three screws. Remove those.

Here's the detail nobody mentions: before you pull the motor out, feel how the blower wheel sits relative to the discharge opening. There's a specific orientation, the wheel needs to sit centered over the port, about 1/8 inch clearance, not pressed against the side. Take a photo on your phone before you pull it. You'll thank yourself when you're putting the new one back.

Disconnect the wire leads. Most are simple push-in connectors or small plug connectors. Slide the old motor out, slide the new one in, reverse the steps. The whole job from pulling the grille to switching the breaker back on runs 30 to 45 minutes if the motor kit is a direct fit.

If the blower wheel is pressed onto the shaft and you need to transfer it to the new motor, you'll need to pull it off. A small gear puller works. A lot of replacement kits include a new wheel, which makes this moot.

The wide range on full fan replacement reflects CFM capacity and features. A basic 50 CFM Broan 688 is around $28. A Panasonic WhisperCeiling FV-11VQ5 at 110 CFM runs about $155. Both are good products, very different use cases.

I've seen people spend $185 on a full fan replacement when a $23 motor kit would have fixed the exact problem. Worked example: a reader emailed me last spring about a loud Delta fan in a master bath. She'd gotten a quote for a full fan swap at $340 installed. Correct fix was a Delta 655 motor kit for $31. She did it herself. Forty minutes. Fan runs quietly.

## When to Replace the Whole Fan Instead

Sometimes the motor isn't the only issue. If the housing is corroded, if it's pulling less than its rated CFM because the ductwork connection is crushed or the housing itself is warped, or if you're upgrading from a 50 CFM to a 110 CFM fan for a larger bathroom, you're into full replacement territory. That's a different article, and it does involve attic access or ceiling work.

One clear tell: if the motor turns freely and the blower wheel spins without wobble, but the fan is exhausting almost no air, the problem is downstream. Check the duct run. I've found flex duct collapsed inside a ceiling that looked fine from the access panel. You're not replacing a motor; you're rerouting ductwork.

Also worth knowing: any fan rated at less than 1.0 sone that you're trying to repair with an aftermarket motor may just run louder than the original spec. Panasonic's WhisperSeries motors are specifically engineered for near-silent operation. The motor drives the sound rating. A generic replacement motor in a Panasonic housing will work, but it'll be louder. That may or may not matter to you.
