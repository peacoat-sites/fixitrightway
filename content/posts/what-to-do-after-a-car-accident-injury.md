You walked away from the fender-bender feeling fine. No broken bones, no blood, airbags didn't even deploy. You exchanged insurance info, maybe took a few photos, and drove home. Then you woke up two days later and couldn't turn your neck. This is the scenario I've seen play out dozens of times, and it's exactly where most people make their most expensive mistakes, both medically and financially. The truth that surprises most homeowners (and really, most people in general) is that what you do in the 72 hours after a car accident matters almost as much as what happened in the collision itself.

## Get Medical Attention Before You Think You Need It

I'll be honest: when I've talked to friends who've been in accidents, the most common thing I hear is "I felt fine, so I didn't go." That instinct is completely understandable and almost completely wrong.

Adrenaline is a real physiological phenomenon, not just a metaphor. In the immediate aftermath of a crash, your body floods itself with stress hormones that genuinely suppress pain signals. Whiplash, soft tissue injuries, and even mild traumatic brain injuries can be completely asymptomatic for 24 to 72 hours. By the time the pain shows up, the window for clean documentation has already started closing.

Go to an emergency room, urgent care, or your primary care physician the same day if you possibly can. If you genuinely can't make it same-day, go first thing the next morning. Tell them you were in a vehicle accident. Be specific about what happened: the direction of impact, whether you hit your head, whether you lost consciousness even briefly. Let them document everything.

What surprised me when I dug into this was how directly the timing of medical care affects insurance claims. Insurers and attorneys both look at the gap between the accident and your first medical visit. A 48-hour gap can be explained. A two-week gap, even if your pain was building the whole time, will be used to argue your injuries weren't caused by the accident.

Keep every single piece of paper: discharge summaries, prescription receipts, referral forms, physical therapy notes. Create a folder, physical or digital, and treat it like it's worth money. Because it is.

## Document Everything at the Scene (If You're Able)

This section assumes you're physically capable of moving around after the accident. If you're not, your only job is to stay still and wait for emergency services.

If you are mobile, the documentation you gather in the first 15 minutes can be worth thousands of dollars later. Here's the order that actually matters:

**Step 1: Call 911.** Even for minor accidents. A police report creates an independent, dated, third-party account of what happened. Without one, it becomes your word against theirs.

**Step 2: Photograph the scene before any vehicles move.** Skid marks, final resting positions of vehicles, traffic signals, road conditions. These details disappear fast.

**Step 3: Photograph all vehicle damage.** Every panel, every angle. Include your license plates in at least one shot so the photos are clearly tied to your specific vehicles.

**Step 4: Get the other driver's information.** Name, phone number, insurance company, policy number, license plate, driver's license number. Photograph their insurance card directly.

**Step 5: Get witness information.** Names and phone numbers. Witnesses leave. Get to them before they do.

**Step 6: Photograph your own body.** Any visible marks, bruising, or lacerations. Do this at the scene and again at home a few hours later when bruising often becomes more visible.

**Step 7: Write down your account.** Not a text, not a voice memo you'll forget to save. A written note with the time, exact location, weather, speed you were traveling, direction of travel, and exactly how the collision happened from your perspective. Memory degrades within hours. Write it while you're still at the scene.

One thing I've learned: don't discuss fault at the scene, even casually. "I didn't even see you" or "I was distracted for a second" feels like honest conversation in the moment. In a legal dispute, it becomes an admission.

## Notify Your Insurance Company Promptly

Call your own insurance company the same day, even if the other driver was clearly at fault. Most policies have a duty to cooperate clause that requires prompt reporting. Failing to do so can complicate your own coverage, particularly if you have uninsured motorist or medical payments coverage you might want to use.

When you call, report the facts: date, time, location, other driver's information. Don't speculate about fault. Don't minimize your injuries by saying "I feel okay" because that statement gets recorded and will come back up.

A common piece of bad advice I see circulating online is "don't contact your insurance if it wasn't your fault." I understand the logic, the worry is that your premiums will go up. But your insurer needs to know, and in many cases your own coverage, like MedPay or PIP (Personal Injury Protection), can pay your medical bills immediately regardless of fault while liability gets sorted out. In no-fault states like Michigan, Florida, and New York, your own insurer is the first stop no matter what.

Be cautious about the other driver's insurance company calling you. They may call quickly, and they will be friendly. Their job is to get a recorded statement while your recollection is fresh and before you have an attorney. You are not required to give one. You can decline politely.

## Understand What Your Injuries Might Actually Cost

The research here is genuinely mixed on average settlement figures because they vary so wildly based on injury severity, state laws, and whether you have an attorney. But I can give you real reference points.

A straightforward whiplash claim with a few months of physical therapy and no surgery typically settles somewhere between $10,000 and $30,000, depending on the state and insurer. Add a herniated disc requiring epidural injections and that range jumps to $50,000 to $150,000 or more. A traumatic brain injury case can reach seven figures.

Where I've seen people leave the most money on the table is in not documenting non-economic damages. These are things like pain and suffering, lost enjoyment of activities, psychological distress, and the impact on your relationships. Keep a daily journal starting immediately after the accident. Note your pain level on a 1-10 scale, what activities you couldn't do that you normally do, how your sleep was affected, how your mood was affected. This journal becomes evidence.

Also track every dollar you spend or lose: mileage to medical appointments (the 2024 IRS medical mileage rate is 21 cents per mile), co-pays, medications, childcare costs necessitated by your injury, and any wages you missed. Keep receipts for everything.

## When to Get an Attorney (And When You Might Not Need One)

I'll be direct about this because the advice online tends to be either "always hire a lawyer" or "insurance companies are fair, just deal with them yourself." Both are oversimplifications.

For minor accidents with no injury, or very minor soft tissue soreness that resolves in a week or two, handling the property damage claim yourself is usually fine. The insurance process for vehicle repairs is relatively straightforward and the amounts involved rarely justify an attorney's contingency fee.

For anything involving:

- Injuries requiring more than a couple of doctor visits
- Any time off work
- Surgery or specialist referrals
- Disputed liability (the other driver is claiming it's your fault)
- Hit and run, uninsured driver, or commercial vehicle
- Any head trauma or neurological symptoms

You should at minimum get a free consultation with a personal injury attorney. Most work on contingency, meaning they take 33% to 40% of the settlement and you pay nothing upfront. Studies consistently show that people represented by attorneys receive significantly higher settlements even after the attorney's fee, often two to three times higher than unrepresented claimants, according to data from the Insurance Research Council.

The consultation costs you nothing and gives you information. There's no reason to skip it.

## What Not to Do (This Is Where Most People Go Wrong)

Do not accept a quick settlement offer without understanding your full medical picture. Insurers sometimes call within days of an accident with settlement offers. They do this because signing a release of all claims before you know whether you need surgery, or whether your symptoms are going to resolve, is a massive win for them and a potential disaster for you. Once you sign, that's it.

Do not post about the accident on social media. Photos of you at a party two weeks after you reported chronic neck pain will be found and used.

Do not miss medical appointments or let gaps develop in your treatment. Gaps in treatment are interpreted as evidence that you weren't really hurt, or that you recovered, even when neither is true.

Do not try to manage a complex injury claim without at least consulting an attorney if significant money is involved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long do I have to file a claim after a car accident injury?

This depends on your state's statute of limitations for personal injury claims, which typically ranges from one to three years from the date of the accident. But don't confuse the statute of limitations with when to start the process. You should notify your insurance company within days, not months. Waiting to document your injuries or seek treatment is always a mistake, even if you technically have years to file a lawsuit.

### What if the other driver has no insurance?

Your own uninsured motorist (UM) coverage kicks in here, assuming you have it, and most states either require it or strongly recommend it. UM coverage pays for your medical bills, lost wages, and pain and suffering damages up to your policy limits. This is exactly why carrying adequate UM coverage matters. The national average for uninsured drivers is around 14%, which is roughly 1 in 7 vehicles on the road.

### Should I see my own doctor or go to an urgent care or ER?

Go wherever you can get seen fastest. Same-day care matters more than which type of facility you choose. ER documentation is slightly more detailed and carries more evidentiary weight in serious injury cases, but urgent care documentation is absolutely valid and widely accepted. What you want to avoid at all costs is having no documentation from the first 24 to 48 hours.

### Can I still file a claim if I didn't get a police report?

Yes, but it's harder. Without a police report you lose that independent third-party account of what happened. To compensate, you'll need strong witness statements, thorough photo documentation, consistent medical records, and potentially dashcam footage. This is a case where having an attorney becomes more important, not less, because you'll need someone who knows how to build the strongest possible case from the evidence you do have.

### What if my pain started days after the accident, not immediately?

Delayed onset of symptoms is medically well-recognized, particularly for soft tissue injuries and concussions. The critical thing is that when symptoms appear, you get medical attention immediately and you explicitly tell your doctor that you were in a vehicle accident on a specific date. That connection has to be in the medical record. The longer you wait to seek care after symptoms begin, the harder it becomes to establish that the accident caused your injury.

---

The moments after a car accident are disorienting and nobody plans for them. But the decisions you make in those first 72 hours, whether you get checked out medically, how well you document the scene, how you communicate with insurers, have a disproportionate effect on everything that comes after. Treat it like a contractor situation I deal with all the time: the people who come out ahead aren't necessarily the ones who got the best luck. They're the ones who took the right steps in the right order before the window closed.
