Most people discover their [kitchen faucet](/how-to-fix-a-leaking-kitchen-faucet-base/) needs replacing at the worst possible moment. Saturday morning, coffee not made yet, and water is either pooling under the sink or spraying somewhere it absolutely shouldn't be. You're standing there wondering if this is a $200 problem or a $1,500 problem, and whether you're about to make it worse.

Here's what I tell people in that moment: replacing a kitchen faucet is one of the most DIY-friendly plumbing jobs there is. I'm not saying that to make you feel capable. I'm saying it because it's genuinely true. No soldering, no cutting pipe, no permits required. A competent first-timer can pull this off in two to three hours. A seasoned DIYer can do it in under ninety minutes. I've replaced probably forty or fifty faucets over the years, from beat-up rentals to high-end kitchen remodels, and the job is almost always the same job.

That said, there are a handful of moments in this project where people make expensive mistakes. I want to walk you through all of it before you touch a wrench.

## What You're Actually Getting Into

Let me give you a realistic picture of the full scope. You're disconnecting two braided supply lines (hot and cold), loosening one or two mounting nuts under the sink, lifting out the old faucet, dropping in the new one, and reconnecting everything. That's the whole job.

The miserable part isn't technically hard. It's physical. You'll be lying on your back inside a cabinet that's sized for cookware, not humans, reaching up into a space you can barely see. If you have a bad back, this is the day it will remind you. A good headlamp (not just your phone flashlight) and a thin piece of cardboard to lie on will make this tolerable rather than genuinely awful. Trust me on the cardboard.

One thing almost nobody tells you: older supply line nuts can be corroded to the point where they won't budge. I've had jobs where the faucet removal took forty-five minutes by itself because of frozen nuts. If your faucet is more than ten years old, spray the supply line connections and the mounting nut with Liquid Wrench or PB Blaster the night before you plan to work. Let it sit overnight. That step has saved me hours.

## Tools and Materials: What You Actually Need

You don't need a plumber's arsenal. Here's the honest list:

A basin wrench is the one thing most people don't own and actually need. It's a long-handled tool with a pivoting jaw designed specifically to reach up into the tight space behind a sink basin to turn mounting nuts. Ridgid makes a solid one for around $25 at Home Depot. Without it, you're trying to reach a nut in a spot where a normal wrench won't fit, and you'll understand very quickly why this tool exists.

Beyond that: an adjustable wrench, channel-lock pliers, a bucket or a few old towels, plumber's tape (Teflon tape, the white roll, about $2), and some penetrating oil if your faucet is older. That's it.

For the faucet itself, as of July 2026, here's a realistic picture of what you're spending across the main categories:

| Builder-grade (functional, basic) | $45 - $90 | Moen Adler, Delta Foundations | Rentals, budget replacements |

Honest opinion: for most people, the $150-$220 range is the sweet spot. Delta and Moen both offer lifetime warranties on their mid-range lines, and the internal cartridges are genuinely better than budget models. I've seen $60 faucets fail in three years; I've seen $180 Deltas run for fifteen. The math isn't complicated.

## Before You Buy: The Hole Configuration Problem

This is where most first-timers make an expensive mistake, and I'll admit I nearly made it myself on a remodel years ago when I was moving fast.

Kitchen sinks come with either one, two, three, or four pre-drilled holes. Your new faucet needs to match what your sink has, or you need a solution for the extras. Single-handle faucets typically need one hole. Traditional two-handle faucets need three holes (one for each handle, one for the spout). Some faucets include a deck plate that covers a three-hole configuration even when the faucet itself only needs one hole, which is useful if you're switching styles.

If your sink has four holes, the fourth is usually for an accessory: a soap dispenser, a sprayer, or a hot water dispenser. You can buy a sink hole cover (also called a sink hole plug) for around $8-$12 if you're not using that extra hole.

Measure before you shop. Take a photo of under your sink so you can see the connections. It takes two minutes and prevents a round trip to the hardware store.

## The Actual Replacement: Step by Step

I'm going to take you through this as if I'm there with you, because the written instructions that come with faucets are often genuinely terrible.

**Step 1: Shut off the water.** Under the sink, you'll find two supply valves (oval or football-shaped handles for compression valves, or flat-head slots for newer ball valves). Turn them clockwise until they stop. Then turn the faucet on to release pressure and confirm water has actually stopped. If those valves are so old they won't close fully, you'll need to shut off water at the main. Don't skip this confirmation step.

**Step 2: Disconnect the supply lines.** Put your bucket under the connections. Use channel-lock pliers or an adjustable wrench to unscrew the supply lines from the valves first (bottom), then from the faucet tail pieces (top). Expect a little residual water to come out. Have towels ready.

**Step 3: Disconnect the sprayer hose** (if your existing faucet has one). It either unscrews or has a clip fitting. Reach up behind the sink body to find the connection point.

**Step 4: Remove the mounting hardware.** This is the basin wrench moment. Lie on your back, reach up behind the sink basin, and use the basin wrench to loosen the mounting nuts holding the faucet body to the sink. On older faucets, this nut may be plastic or metal. Loosen counterclockwise. If it's frozen, this is where that penetrating oil from the night before earns its keep.

**Step 5: Lift out the old faucet.** Once the mounting nut is off and the supply lines are disconnected, the faucet lifts straight up out of the hole. Clean off any old putty or caulk residue on the sink surface before you proceed.

**Step 6: Install the new faucet.** Most modern faucets have a rubber or plastic deck plate gasket that seals against the sink. If yours doesn't, you'll apply a thin bead of plumber's putty around the base instead. Thread the supply lines and any weight/hose assemblies down through the mounting hole, set the faucet body in place, and go back underneath to hand-tighten the mounting nut. Then snug it with the basin wrench, firm, not gorilla-tight. You want it secure, but these are not lug nuts.

**Step 7: Connect the supply lines.** If your new faucet came with supply lines (most do), use those. Wrap the threads on the faucet tail pieces with two layers of Teflon tape (clockwise direction), hand-tighten the connections, then snug them with pliers. Again: firm, not brutal. The most common post-installation drip I see comes from either no tape or over-tightened plastic fittings that crack.

**Step 8: Turn the water back on slowly** and watch every connection as pressure builds. A small drip at a connection just means a quarter-turn more with the wrench. Let water run for a minute and confirm everything is dry and the faucet operates properly.

Here's a scenario that plays out often:

Older home, original faucet from the early 2000s, three-hole configuration → homeowner buys a single-handle Delta Leland with a deck plate ($189 at Home Depot), uses existing supply lines, spends $25 on a basin wrench → total project cost: $214, completed in two and a half hours, zero callbacks.

Compare that to a plumber visit: in most markets [right now](/how-to-shop-for-materials-right-now-and-beat-tariff-price-hikes/), a plumber will charge $150-$250 in labor alone to swap a faucet, on top of whatever the faucet costs. For a job this size, that math rarely makes sense unless you run into a real complication.

## When to Actually Call a Pro

I want to be straight with you here. Most kitchen faucet replacements don't need a plumber. But some do, and recognizing the line matters.

Call a plumber if: you discover the supply valves under the sink are so corroded they won't close (this is now a valve replacement job, which involves soldering or compression fittings on the main supply line). Call one if you find evidence of [water damage](/how-to-repair-drywall-water-damage/) -- soft flooring under the cabinet, dark staining on the cabinet floor, or visible mold. Those are signs of a longer-running problem that the faucet swap won't fix.

Also call if you're switching from a single-hole to a three-hole setup and your sink doesn't have the extra holes. Drilling porcelain or composite granite sinks requires a diamond-tipped hole saw and a steady hand; one crack and you've turned a $200 project into a sink replacement.

Everything else? You've got this.
