Labor costs are up, YouTube tutorials are better than ever, and 55% of homeowners now plan to handle their own repairs rather than hire out. The DIY boom makes complete sense. What's getting glossed over is the trap waiting on the other side: unpermitted work is now one of the most common reasons insurance claims get denied, and the consequences go well beyond a slap on the wrist.

This isn't a niche problem. Estimates suggest up to 50% of U.S. homes have some form of unpermitted work sitting inside them right now. Most of those homeowners have no idea they're exposed.

## Unpermitted Electrical Work Is the Specific Thing Killing Claims

Home inspectors flagged unpermitted electrical work as the leading DIY issue behind denied homeowners insurance claims, according to reporting from Homedit in April 2026. Not structural additions. Not plumbing. Electrical.

That tracks. Electrical is where the consequences of skipping a permit are most catastrophic and most visible to an adjuster. A house fire traced back to a DIY panel upgrade or a homeowner-installed subpanel without inspection gives an insurer clean grounds to deny the claim entirely. The unpermitted work doesn't have to be the direct cause of the damage, either. Discovery alone can trigger policy cancellation, premium increases, or a reduced payout that leaves you holding the rest of the bill.

The sequence usually goes like this: something goes wrong, you file a claim, the insurer sends an adjuster, the adjuster notices work that doesn't match permitted records, and suddenly your $80,000 kitchen fire is partially or fully your problem. Econosurance's April 2026 analysis spells out all three outcomes: reduced payouts, premium hikes, and outright cancellation. Any one of those hurts. The combination is devastating.

## California Just Changed the Rules Mid-Project for a Lot of DIYers

If you're in California and pulled a permit in January 2026 or later, you're now subject to the 2025 Building Energy Efficiency Standards. These took effect on January 1, 2026, and they're not minor tweaks. Heat pump requirements expanded significantly, and ventilation rules tightened in ways that directly affect HVAC work, water heater replacements, and kitchen and bathroom renovations.

The timing caught a lot of DIYers mid-project. Someone who bought a water heater in December expecting to swap it out in February suddenly found out the replacement they planned doesn't meet the new standards once a permit application triggered the new code requirements. The permit isn't optional here, which means the new standards aren't optional either.

Other states are moving in the same direction, just slower. If you're planning any mechanical work this year, check your local jurisdiction's current adopted code before you buy equipment, not after.

## The Math on Getting Caught Has Changed

Fines for unpermitted work run $100 to $5,000 per violation depending on the municipality, and that's before you factor in stop-work orders or required tearout of completed work. Yes, tearout. Local building departments have the authority to make you rip out finished walls to expose work that was never inspected.

Retroactive permitting, when it's even available, runs $5,000 to $50,000 or more depending on scope, according to March 2026 data from PLHH. And it's not just a code enforcement risk. Most mortgage lenders in 2026 flag unpermitted work during underwriting, which means if you try to sell or refinance, that DIY deck or finished basement becomes a transaction problem. Buyers' agents are trained to spot it. Inspectors are specifically looking for it. The days of quietly passing unpermitted work from one owner to the next are getting harder.

The financial exposure looks like this, ranked from annoying to catastrophic: municipal fines, failed sale or refinance, retroactive permitting costs, denied insurance claim, total policy cancellation. Most people who skip permits are mentally pricing in the first one and ignoring the rest.

## What Actually Requires a Permit (That Surprises People)

The common misconception is that permits are for major structural work only. The real list is longer and more specific than most homeowners expect.

Electrical panel upgrades, new circuits, and most subpanel work almost universally require permits. So do load-bearing wall removals, deck construction above a certain height, HVAC system replacements (especially in code-updated states), and in many jurisdictions, water heater swaps. Bathroom additions, finished basements, and any work that changes the home's square footage or use classification trigger permits in virtually every municipality.

What generally doesn't require one: painting, flooring, cabinet swaps without electrical or plumbing changes, most fixture replacements, and cosmetic work that doesn't touch structure or systems. If you're in doubt, call your local building department. That phone call costs nothing and takes ten minutes. The permit itself is often $50 to $500, which is cheap insurance compared to the alternative.

## How to Protect Yourself Going Forward

If you've done unpermitted work already, the worst move is doing nothing. An inspector, an insurance adjuster, or a buyer's agent will eventually find it, and discovering it on your terms is always better than having someone else discover it on theirs.

Start by pulling your permit history through your local building department's online portal, most municipalities have one now. Compare what's on record against what's in your house. If there are gaps, consult with a contractor about whether retroactive permitting is possible in your jurisdiction and what remediation would cost. That number might be uncomfortable, but it's a real number. The surprise version, arriving during a claim or a sale, is worse.

Going forward, the permit question should come before the project plan, not after. Budget it in like materials. If a project genuinely requires one and you're going the DIY route, pull it yourself. Most jurisdictions allow homeowners to act as their own general contractor and pull permits for their own residence. The inspection process is also free education. A building inspector walking through your work before it's closed up catches mistakes that would otherwise stay hidden until they cost you something.

The DIY calculation still pencils out on plenty of projects. It just has to include the full cost, and right now, a lot of people are leaving the permit line blank.
