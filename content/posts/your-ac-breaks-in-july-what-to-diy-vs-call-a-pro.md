Most AC breakdown coverage goes straight to "call your HVAC tech." That advice will cost you $300 before anyone opens your unit, and in July 2026, with technicians slammed by heat wave demand, you might wait two days for that privilege. The smarter move is a 20-minute triage before you dial anyone.

Here's what's actually happening out there right now: full system replacements are running $7,000 to $20,000 for an average 2,000-square-foot home, according to Modernize data pulled from 56,000 real homeowner projects. Federal efficiency mandates that took effect this year mean a "simple swap" often triggers required ductwork upgrades and electrical panel work, adding thousands you didn't budget. Refrigerant rules are biting harder too, especially if your system runs R-22. And labor shortages are pushing emergency same-day fees to $100 to $300 on top of whatever the repair itself costs. So yes, the stakes for getting this triage right are higher than they've ever been.

## Start Here: The Free Fixes That Actually Work

Before you do anything else, check three things. First, the air filter. A clogged filter starves your system of airflow, which causes the evaporator coil to ice over, which shuts the whole unit down. Looks like a breakdown. Isn't one. Swap in a new $15 filter and give the system 30 minutes to thaw.

Second, the breaker. AC units trip breakers, especially during the first heat wave of the season when demand spikes suddenly. Go to your panel, find the breaker labeled for your air handler or condenser, flip it fully off then back on. If it trips again immediately, stop there and call a pro. That means something's drawing too much current.

Third, the thermostat. Swap the batteries. This sounds too simple to matter, and it is, in fact, how a meaningful percentage of "broken AC" service calls end. New batteries, system kicks back on, everyone feels mildly embarrassed except the homeowner who just saved $300.

These three steps cost almost nothing and resolve a surprising share of mid-summer calls.

## When You've Got a Real Problem: Knowing What You're Dealing With

If the free fixes didn't work, you need to figure out what category of problem you have before you talk to anyone. This matters because the repair-versus-replace math depends entirely on it.

| Problem | Typical Repair Cost | DIY? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dirty/clogged filter | $10–$20 | Yes |
| Dead thermostat batteries | $5 | Yes |
| Tripped breaker (one-time) | $0 | Yes |
| Capacitor replacement | $150–$400 | No |
| Refrigerant recharge (R-410A) | $200–$400 | No – requires EPA 608 cert |
| Refrigerant recharge (R-22) | $400–$800+ | No – R-22 is scarce and regulated |
| Compressor replacement | $1,500–$2,800+ | No |
| Full system replacement | $7,000–$20,000 | No |

The capacitor is actually the most common non-filter, non-thermostat failure. It's a small cylindrical component that helps the motors start. Symptoms are the outdoor unit humming but not spinning, or short-cycling. The part itself is $15 to $50. But capacitors hold a charge that can kill you even after the power is off. This is not a DIY repair, despite what YouTube will tell you.

## The R-22 Trap: Don't Get Ambushed

If your system is more than 15 years old, there's a real chance it runs on R-22 refrigerant, the stuff that was phased out under Clean Air Act regulations. Replenishing even a few pounds now costs $200 to $400 in refrigerant alone, per current market pricing reported by Spectrum News 1, because supply is essentially scavenged from recovered stockpiles. Some techs won't touch it at all.

A low refrigerant charge always means a leak somewhere. Topping it off without fixing the leak is money poured into a system that will fail again in weeks. If your tech quotes you an R-22 recharge without mentioning a leak search, find a different tech.

The honest math: if your R-22 system needs a refrigerant recharge plus a compressor, you're looking at $2,000 to $3,600 or more, and you still own an aging, inefficient system that runs on unobtainium refrigerant. That's the scenario where replacement pencils out.

## How to Avoid Getting Upsold on a Full Replacement

Replacement is absolutely the right call sometimes. It's also the highest-margin sale in a tech's day. Here's how to keep the conversation honest.

Get the repair cost in writing before authorizing anything. A legitimate tech will give you a written estimate. If the repair quote is less than 50% of replacement cost and your system is under 12 years old, repair is almost always the right answer. If the repair quote is over 50% of replacement and the system is 15-plus years old, the replacement math starts to make sense, but you should still get a second opinion on a quote that size.

The 2026 efficiency mandates are real and they do affect replacement costs. As CBS News reported in May 2026, homeowners expecting a straightforward equipment swap are sometimes finding they need panel upgrades and duct modifications to meet new efficiency requirements, which can add $1,000 to $3,000 to the job. A reputable contractor explains exactly what the mandates require for your specific situation. Vague references to "new code requirements" without specifics is a yellow flag.

Also, emergency dispatch fees are negotiable more often than homeowners assume. If you're not in immediate danger (no elderly residents, no one with a medical condition, the house isn't 105 degrees), waiting 24 to 48 hours for a non-emergency appointment can save you $100 to $300 in dispatch premiums, according to current service call pricing tracked by Call Home Rangers.

## When You Cannot Wait: The Conditions That Change the Calculus

Heat is dangerous. If you have elderly family members, infants, or anyone with a cardiovascular or respiratory condition in the house, the math on waiting changes completely. In that situation, get them somewhere air-conditioned first, then deal with the repair on a normal timeline. A hotel for two nights is cheaper than a compressor replacement and far cheaper than a medical emergency.

Signs that require a pro same-day, no triage delay: refrigerant hissing from a line, burning smell from any component, breaker that trips repeatedly after reset, water actively pouring from the air handler. None of these are wait-and-see situations.
