Roughly 60% of American homes with basements will develop a crack in the foundation wall. That number comes from the American Society of Home Inspectors, and every time I cite it on a job site, homeowners look at me like I just told them something was wrong with their house specifically. It's not specific. It's just that common.

You might be wondering whether your crack is the kind you can handle yourself over a weekend or the kind that means calling a structural engineer before you touch anything. That's the right question, and the answer depends on things most DIY articles skip entirely: the crack's orientation, whether it's moving, and what's happening on the other side of that wall. I'll walk you through all of it.

I've sealed hundreds of foundation cracks over the past two decades, in basements from Chicago suburb colonials to Pacific Northwest split-levels. Most of them were straightforward. A handful were not. The difference between those two categories is almost always something a homeowner could have identified early, before a $200 repair turned into a $12,000 excavation.

## What Your Crack Is Actually Telling You

Not all foundation cracks are the same problem in different sizes. They're often completely different problems.

Vertical cracks are the most common. They usually form as concrete cures and shrinks, or as the house settles over its first several years. A narrow vertical crack (under 1/8 inch) with no displacement and no water intrusion is almost always low-risk. You can seal it yourself.

Diagonal cracks, typically running at 30 to 75 degrees from horizontal, often start at a corner of a window or door opening. These signal differential settlement: one part of your foundation is moving slightly differently than another. A single diagonal crack that's been stable for years is usually manageable. Multiple diagonal cracks, or one that's actively widening, is a different story.

Horizontal cracks are the ones that make me sit a homeowner down and have a direct conversation. These form when lateral soil pressure from outside pushes against the wall harder than the concrete can resist. In block foundations, you'll sometimes see a stair-step pattern through the [mortar joints](/how-to-repair-crumbling-brick-mortar-joints/) that signals the same thing. Here's what I tell people: a horizontal crack doesn't mean your house is about to fall down, but it means the wall is already in some degree of bending stress, and sealing it yourself does nothing to address that. Call a structural engineer. A one-hour consultation typically runs $300–$600, and it's worth every dollar.

Stair-step cracks in block or brick foundations often indicate settlement in a specific corner. They can be DIY-repairable if minor, but the underlying drainage or soil issue usually needs attention too.

One detail that rarely makes it into online guides: run your finger along the crack and feel whether one side is slightly raised relative to the other. Even 1/16 inch of displacement means the two sides of the wall have moved in different planes. That changes the repair method and, sometimes, whether you should repair it yourself at all.

## The Products That Actually Work (And the Ones That Don't)

I made a mistake on my third basement job, back when I was still learning. I used hydraulic cement to fill an active seeping crack, packed it tight, and called it done. Six months later the homeowner called me back. The water had found a path around the patch. Hydraulic cement stops active gushing water temporarily, but it doesn't bond well to wet concrete and it's not flexible. For a static, dry crack it's fine as a filler. For a leaking crack, you need something that bonds to damp surfaces and moves with the concrete.

There are three products you'll actually be choosing between:

**Polyurethane foam injection** is the gold standard for active water leaks. It expands on contact with water (some formulas expand 20 to 30 times their volume), fills every void in the crack, and stays flexible. It does not restore structural strength. Sika's Injection-201 and Simpson Strong-Tie's ET-HP are both products I've used with good results. Expect to pay $80–$130 for a DIY kit that covers a crack up to about 8 feet.

**Epoxy injection** bonds the two sides of a crack together under pressure and, once cured, is actually stronger than the surrounding concrete. It's the right call for dry, dormant cracks where you want to restore structural integrity. It does not work in actively wet conditions. United States Concrete Repair's Epoxy Injection System and Rhino Carbon Fiber's crack repair kits are solid options. These kits run $90–$150.

**Hydraulic cement** (Quikrete makes the most common version) is a fast-setting patching material for temporarily stopping active water flow before a more permanent repair. Think of it as buying yourself time.

A word on those elastomeric paint-on sealers you see at big box stores: they're fine for controlling mild moisture vapor transmission on an otherwise dry wall, but they are not a crack repair. If water is actively getting through a crack, painting over it will fail within a season.

## How to Actually Do the Repair (Polyurethane Foam Injection, Step by Step)

This is the method for a leaking vertical crack. It's the repair most homeowners are actually facing, and it's genuinely doable in an afternoon if you have the right kit and go slowly.

You'll need: the polyurethane injection kit (comes with ports, surface paste, injection gun, and cartridges), a wire brush, shop vac, acetone or denatured alcohol, and safety glasses. The entire process takes about 3–4 hours including cure time before injection.

Start by cleaning the crack aggressively. Wire brush out any loose concrete, vacuum the dust, then wipe the surface with acetone. The surface paste won't bond to a dusty wall, and I've seen homeowners skip this step only to have the whole port system pull off during injection. Takes ten minutes. Don't skip it.

Space your injection ports every 8 to 12 inches along the crack, centered over it. Wider spacing for shallow cracks; closer for cracks you can see are deep. Mix the surface paste (two-part epoxy, usually) and seal the crack between ports entirely, leaving just the port connections open. Let that cure for the manufacturer-specified time, usually 20 to 30 minutes.

Inject starting from the lowest port. Slow, steady pressure. Stop when foam starts weeping from the next port up, then cap the lower port and move up. You're filling from the bottom of the crack toward the top, letting gravity help you reach every void. The whole thing moves slowly, and that's correct. Rushing injection is how you get voids.

Wait 24 hours before removing ports and grinding the surface patch flush. One detail nobody tells you: the foam will sometimes bleed out slightly on the exterior side if the crack goes through the full wall thickness. That's actually a good sign. It means you filled it completely.

**Worked examples:**

Homeowner in suburban Columbus, Ohio noticed water streaking down a 6-foot vertical crack in her poured concrete wall every time it rained. She purchased a Sika Injection-201 kit for $112, spent a Saturday on the repair, and the crack has stayed dry through two full rainy seasons. Total cost: $112 and one afternoon.

A reader in Portland, Oregon emailed me last spring about a crack he'd tried to seal twice with hydraulic cement only to have it reopen. He switched to polyurethane foam injection, and more importantly found that the crack was actually 1/4 inch wide at the exterior (only appeared narrow from inside). Professional repair with pressure injection from outside ran $1,847 but included a 10-year warranty. The cement patches had cost him $280 total over two attempts.

A homeowner in Atlanta discovered three diagonal cracks converging near a corner after a particularly dry summer followed by heavy rains. A structural engineer found that a downspout had been discharging against the foundation for years. Correcting the drainage cost $340; the crack repairs ran $780 through a waterproofing contractor. Without fixing the drainage first, any sealant would have been temporary. Total spend to get it right: $1,120.

## When to Stop and Call Someone

I'll be direct here, because I think some DIY content is irresponsible on this point. You should not attempt to DIY the repair, or let it sit, if:

The crack is horizontal or shows stair-step pattern in block with visible inward bowing. Any displacement (one side is higher or lower than the other). The crack has grown noticeably over the past 6 to 12 months. You see multiple cracks forming a pattern rather than one isolated crack. There's water intrusion combined with efflorescence (white mineral deposits) on a large area of the wall, suggesting water is moving through a large zone rather than a discrete crack.

Efflorescence is actually a useful diagnostic. A concentrated white deposit along one crack means water is coming through that crack. Efflorescence spread over a large area of wall means you may have a permeability issue across the whole wall, not just that crack. These are different problems.

The American Concrete Institute (ACI) publishes guidance in ACI 224.1R on concrete crack repair that differentiates structural from non-structural repairs; it's worth knowing that standard exists even if you never read it.
